Every Saturday, the same seven questions.

I've answered them so many times that my husband says I should print them on the back of my sign. So here they are, all in one place, answered honestly. If you've ever stood at a market table and wondered about any of them, this is for you.

I'm Emma, by the way. Forty-two, small town in Pennsylvania, two kids, one golden retriever named Biscuit who would like it on the record that he has never once been allowed to work the table.

Question one: "What are they made of?"

Flour, salt, warm water, a few drops of essential oil and a pressed flower. That's it.

People never believe me. They expect resin, or some kind of clay from a craft store, or something with a long chemical name. When I tell them it's basically what's in their pantry, their whole face relaxes. Moms especially. Because now it can go in the baby's room, or next to the dog's bed, and nobody has to worry.

That one answer sells more stones than anything else I say all day.

Question two: "How long does the smell last?"

Six to ten weeks, depending on where you put it. A closet holds the scent longer than a sunny windowsill.

And when it fades, you don't throw it away. Two drops of oil and it's back. I refill them for free at my table, which means my customers come back to find me every couple of months. Some come back with the stone in their coat pocket like a lucky charm. Most of them leave with three more.

Question three: "Did you really make these yourself?"

Yes. At my kitchen table, usually after the kids are asleep, with a show on in the background. A hundred stones takes me about three evenings.

What I don't usually say, because there's a line behind them, is that I'm not a crafty person. I can't sew. I can't paint. My first batches came out grey, lumpy and cracked, and the smell was gone in a week. What changed wasn't talent. It was learning a handful of very specific things. How warm the water should be. How long to knead. What keeps them white. Where the oil goes so it lasts. Small stuff, but it's the difference between something you'd hide in a drawer and something a stranger pays for.

Question four: "How much are they?"

People always ask this one a little quietly, like they're bracing for it.

Twelve dollars for one. Thirty for three. Most people take three, because they already know one's going to be a gift.

And yes, I know what you're thinking, because the woman who asks this question sometimes asks the follow-up too: "What do they cost you?" About fifty cents each. I don't hide it. It's the reason this works for someone like me, who didn't have money to put into a business.

Question five: "Do you sell them in stores?"

Three of them, now. A hair salon, a coffee shop and a little gift store downtown. They buy wholesale for six dollars a stone and sell them at twelve.

None of that came from me knocking on doors. The salon owner bought three at a market, put one at her front desk, and her clients kept asking about it. The coffee shop was the same story. The stones did the pitching. I just showed up with a box.

Question six: "Can I order them for my shower / my wedding / my office?"

Yes, and this is the part of the business people don't see. Half of what I sell never touches a market table. It's orders that come in on WhatsApp all week. Forty favors for a baby shower. Twelve gift boxes for a dentist's office at Christmas. A hundred for a wedding in June, with the couple's initial pressed into each one.

The market is where people meet me. WhatsApp is where they come back.

Question seven.

This one never comes while there's a line. It comes at the end of the day, when I'm packing up, from a woman who's been standing a little to the side for a while. She waits until nobody else is listening, and then she asks it.

"Do you think I could do this?"

I've been asked that question by a nurse, a retired teacher, a woman who'd just gone through a divorce, a nineteen-year-old who was saving for a car, and a grandmother who wanted to pay for her grandson's summer camp.

My answer is always yes. Not to be nice. Because I know exactly what it takes, and it's not much. A kitchen table. A few evenings. Some pantry ingredients. And the specific know-how that took me months of ruined batches to work out.

That last part is the only thing I can actually hand to somebody. So I wrote it down.

It's called The Scented Stones Method. It's short and it's practical. It's the formula I use, step by step, plus all the little fixes for cracking, color and scent. How I package them so they look like they came from a boutique. How I price singles, three-packs and gift sets. How I talk to shops. And the messages I send on WhatsApp, word for word, so you're not staring at your phone wondering what to say.

It costs less than a three-pack at my table. Dirt cheap, on purpose. I wanted the woman asking question seven to be able to say yes to herself without thinking about it.

So if you've ever wanted to ask me question seven, this is my answer.

I'm Emma. I'm forty-two, I live in Pennsylvania, and I've been selling handmade scented stones for about a year and a half. Most markets go well. This one didn't. Here's exactly what went wrong.

Mistake one: wrong crowd.

It was listed as a "spring market." It turned out to be a vintage car show with a few vendor tables on the side. Hundreds of people came. Almost all of them were men in their sixties looking at engines. Lovely people. Not one of them needed something for their bathroom.

Mistake two: wrong spot.

I arrived late, so I got the last space, at the far end, right next to the portable toilets. Which is a terrible place to sell something whose entire selling point is that it smells nice.

Mistake three: nothing to smell.

Usually, I keep one stone out of the box, freshly oiled, and I hand it to everyone who slows down. "Smell this." That's my whole pitch, and it works almost every time. That day, I was so flustered from being late that I forgot. Everything stayed in its box. People walked by and saw little white rocks.

Mistake four: I hid behind the table.

I sat down. I looked at my phone. I didn't make eye contact. By noon, I'd decided it was a bad day, so I acted like it was a bad day, and it became one.

Mistake five: I brought the wrong scents.

I'd made almost half of them in eucalyptus mint, because I liked it. The truth is, lavender and vanilla outsell everything else at my table about three to one. On a slow day, you want what people already love, not what you happen to love.

Four stones. Forty-two dollars, including one three-pack bought by a man for his wife, who I could have hugged.

I drove home feeling like an idiot. I sat in the driveway and I seriously thought about quitting.

And here's the part that changed my mind, and the reason I'm telling you this story.

The next morning, I still had a hundred and six stones.

If I'd been selling cookies, or cupcakes, or anything that spoils, that would have been a disaster. Money in the trash.

So that Monday, I did three things.

I took twenty to the hair salon that already sells mine, and the owner bought all of them at wholesale, six dollars each, because her clients had been asking for the new scents.

And I messaged the women who'd ordered from me before.

By Wednesday night, all hundred and six were gone.

The worst market I ever did ended up being one of my best weeks.
