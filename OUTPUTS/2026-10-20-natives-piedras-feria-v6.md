DESCRIPCIÓN
People ask me all the time how much I make at a market.

Usually I dodge it. I say something like "It depends," and change the subject, because talking about money makes me uncomfortable.

But last Saturday, I decided to write down every single dollar. What I spent, what I sold, and how many hours it actually took. That's the notebook in the photo, on my kitchen table at eleven at night, with my cold tea next to it.

I'm going to share the whole list with you. Not because it's impressive, but because when I was trying to figure out if this was worth it, nobody would give me real numbers. Just "you can make so much!" with a smiley face. That never helped me.

So here's the honest version.

I'm Emma. I'm forty-two, I live in a small town in Pennsylvania, and I sell handmade scented stones. Before this, I worked part-time at a flower shop for thirteen dollars an hour.

What I spent

Booth fee: thirty-five dollars. This is a mid-size Saturday market, about two hundred visitors on a normal day.

Materials for sixty-four stones: about thirty-two dollars. Flour, salt, essential oils, dried flowers. Roughly fifty cents a stone.

Kraft boxes, twine and little thank-you cards: fifteen dollars.

Gas: twelve dollars, round trip.

Coffee and a blueberry muffin at nine in the morning, because I'm human: six dollars.

Total spent: one hundred dollars, almost exactly.

What I sold

I brought sixty-four stones. I came home with zero.

Eighteen people bought three-packs at thirty dollars each. That's fifty-four stones, five hundred and forty dollars.

Ten people bought a single stone at twelve dollars. That's one hundred and twenty dollars.

Total sold: six hundred and sixty dollars.

What I kept

Six hundred sixty, minus one hundred. Five hundred and sixty dollars.

How long it took

This is the part nobody ever tells you, so I counted carefully.

Making sixty-four stones took me two evenings at the kitchen table, about two and a half hours each, with a show on. Five hours.

Packing them into boxes on Friday night: one hour.

The market itself, from loading the car at seven to getting home at two: seven hours.

Total: thirteen hours.

Five hundred and sixty dollars divided by thirteen hours is a little over forty-three dollars an hour.

At the flower shop, I made thirteen.

I sat at the table for a while after I did that math.

What the list doesn't show

There are three things that aren't in the notebook, and honestly, they matter more than the five hundred sixty.

One. I handed out nine little cards with my WhatsApp number. By Tuesday, three of those women had messaged me. One wants twenty-four stones for her daughter's baby shower. One wants a gift box for her mother-in-law. One just wants "more of the vanilla ones, please." None of that money was made at the market, but all of it came from the market.

Two. A woman who owns a small boutique two towns over stopped by, bought a three-pack, and asked if I sell wholesale. I do, at six dollars a stone. She's taking forty to start.

Three. The stones that sell at the market keep selling for me after. Every one of them goes into a bathroom or a closet or a car, and someone else smells it, and asks. That's free advertising, every single week.

The honest part

Not every Saturday looks like this. In January, there are no markets near me, and I live on WhatsApp orders and the shops. My first few markets, I brought too few stones, priced them too low, and came home with a lot less. One market was so bad I sold four. It happens.

But this is what a normal, decent Saturday looks like now, about a year and a half in. Not a miracle. Not "quit your job tomorrow." Just a very good use of thirteen hours.

How I started

When I started, my budget was less than thirty dollars. A bag of flour, a box of salt, one bottle of lavender oil and a silicone mold from the craft store. That was the whole investment.

What I didn't have was the know-how. My first batches were awful. Grey, lumpy, cracked, and they smelled like nothing within a week. It took me months of ruined trays to figure out the details that make them sell. How to get them smooth and white. Why they crack and how to stop it. How much oil to use, and when, so they last for weeks. How to box them so they look like a boutique gift instead of a school project.

Eventually I put all of it into a short guide called The Scented Stones Method. The formula, the fixes, the packaging, the pricing for singles and three-packs and wholesale, and the exact WhatsApp messages I send after a market.

It's the cheapest line in my notebook. Less than my booth fee.

Here's what I'd suggest, if you're on the fence. Grab a notebook and write your own list. What you'd spend, what you might sell, how many hours you have. Be honest, like I tried to be.

DESCRIPCIÓN
I keep a note on my phone called "Things people say at my table."

I started it as a joke, after a market where three different people asked me the same strange question. Now it's over two hundred lines long, and it's honestly my favorite thing I own.

Here are some of the best ones, and what each of them taught me about this little business.

"Is this soap?"

At least ten times every market. Usually followed by someone trying to sniff it and then rub it between their hands.

What it taught me: people have never seen these before. That's the best position a seller can be in. You're not competing with anybody. You're introducing something.

"Can I eat it?"

A five-year-old named Mason, holding a lavender stone with both hands like it was a cookie. That's his tiny hand in the photo. His mom laughed so hard she bought three.

What it taught me: kids pull their parents to my table. The stones look like candy, and they're made of flour and salt, so nobody panics when a little one picks one up.

"My mother used to make these."

An older woman, maybe eighty, who stood very still with a stone in her hand. She told me her mother made something similar in Portugal, with flour and rosemary, to keep in the linen closet. She hadn't smelled anything like it in sixty years.

She bought one. She didn't want a box. She put it straight into her coat pocket and patted it.

What it taught me: I'm not just selling a nice smell. Sometimes I'm selling a memory.

"I'm buying this for my ex-husband's new wife."

A woman in sunglasses, completely deadpan. I laughed so hard I nearly dropped the tray. Then she said, "We get along, actually. She has great taste." And bought her the gift box with the bow.

What it taught me: almost every customer is buying for someone else. These are gifts first, home decor second. That's why I always have boxes ready.

"Are these imported? They look European."

A man in a tweed jacket, turning one over to look for a label. I told him the idea came from my aunt in Italy, but this one was made on Tuesday at my kitchen table. He looked genuinely disappointed for a second, and then bought two anyway.

What it taught me: presentation does half the work. A smooth white stone, a pressed flower and a kraft box with twine look like they came from a shop in Florence.

"Do you take Venmo?"

Every few minutes. I take cards, cash and Venmo, and I keep a sign up so nobody has to ask.

What it taught me: never lose a sale over something small.

"I need one for my mom's hospital room."

A woman in scrubs, on her way to visit. She said the room smelled like bleach, and her mom kept saying she missed her garden. She bought a rose stone. Two weeks later she came back and bought a three-pack for the nurses on the floor.

I think about her a lot.

What it taught me: there is real comfort in a small thing that smells good. People will find reasons for these that I'd never think of.

"This is the third time I've walked past. I can't stop thinking about the vanilla one."

I hear this one surprisingly often. People circle back.

What it taught me: always have a stone out, freshly oiled, so they can smell it again on their second lap. The second smell sells.

"Wait, they're how much? That's it?"

Most people expect them to cost more. They look like boutique pieces. They're twelve dollars, or three for thirty, and most people walk away with three.

And they cost me about fifty cents each to make, which still surprises me when I say it out loud.

What it taught me: price it like a gift, not like a craft. People are happy to pay for something that looks special.

"How do you make them?"

This is the one I hear the most. Usually quietly, at the end of the day, from a woman who's been hanging around the side of the table for a while.

I always tell the truth. They're made from things in your pantry. Flour, salt, warm water, essential oil and dried flowers, pressed into a mold at the kitchen table and left to dry. No oven, no special equipment.

But I also tell them the other truth. My first batches were a disaster. They cracked, came out grey and stopped smelling within a week. It took me months to figure out the little details that make them good enough to sell. And I wish someone had just handed me those details at the beginning.

So I wrote them down. The Scented Stones Method is a short, practical guide. The formula I use, all the fixes for cracking and color, how to make the scent last for weeks, how to package them like a boutique gift, how to price them, and the messages I send to customers and shops, word for word.

It's dirt cheap. Less than one three-pack at my table.

A year and a half ago, I didn't have any of these lines in my phone. I didn't have a table. I worked part-time at a flower shop in my small town in Pennsylvania, and our money ran out about a week before the month did.

Now, every Saturday, strangers tell me stories I'll never forget. And I get to go home and pay the bills with something I made with my hands.

My wife asked me to write this one.

She says people believe the husband more. I don't know if that's true. But she's usually right about these things, so here I am, typing on her laptop at the kitchen table, which is covered in flour, as usual.

My name is Michael. I'm a mechanic. I've been married to Emma for nineteen years, and we live in a small town in Pennsylvania with our two kids and a golden retriever named Biscuit, who is the one in the picture, supervising.

I want to tell you what this looked like from my side. Because I wasn't the supportive husband at the beginning. I want to be honest about that.

When Emma came back from visiting her aunt in Italy and started making these little stones, I thought it was a hobby. Then I thought it was a phase. Then, when I came home one night and every surface in the kitchen was covered in trays, and the trash can was full of cracked ones, I thought it was a waste of flour.

I said that out loud, actually. "That's a lot of wasted flour." I'm not proud of it.

She didn't say anything. She just kept going.

What I didn't understand then was how bad things felt for her. I was working overtime most Saturdays. She was at the flower shop part-time. And every month, our money ran out a week before the month did. I knew it was tight. What I didn't see was that she was the one opening the bills, moving money around, deciding what we could skip. She carried that by herself for a long time.

These stones were her way out of that. I just didn't know it yet.

It took her months to get them right. I watched it happen without really paying attention. The grey ones turned white. The cracked ones stopped cracking. One night she held one under my nose and asked, "Does this still smell?" It was three weeks old. It smelled like a garden.

That's when I started paying attention.

The real turning point was a Tuesday night. I came in from the garage and found our daughter at the kitchen table next to Emma, pressing dried flowers into the stones with a toothpick, both of them laughing about something. Our son was folding kraft boxes. The radio was on. Nobody was on a phone.

I stood in the doorway for a minute and thought, when was the last time this kitchen felt like this? I honestly couldn't remember.

I washed my hands, sat down, and asked what I could do. Emma handed me a box of twine and said, "Don't make it ugly." I made it ugly. She kept me anyway.

Her first market, she went alone, because I was working. She took sixty stones in a cardboard box. She called me at 10:30 in the morning, and I thought something was wrong because she was crying.

They'd sold out.

I left work early that day. First time in about four years.

Here's what I do now. Saturday mornings, I load the car. Tent, tablecloth, trays, boxes. Sometimes I drive, and I stand at the end of the table and wrap gift boxes, badly. Sunday nights, I help pack the orders that came in on WhatsApp during the week. I've learned more about lavender versus eucalyptus than any mechanic should know.

I asked her to put the numbers in here, because I'm the kind of guy who wants to see numbers.

Each stone costs her about fifty cents to make. She sells them for twelve dollars, or three for thirty. Shops in town buy them wholesale for six. She makes them at the kitchen table in the evenings, after the kids go to bed, two or three nights a week.

What it's done for us is simple to explain. I don't work Saturday overtime anymore. I go to my son's games. Our money doesn't run out before the month does. And my wife doesn't sit at the kitchen table at midnight moving numbers around with that look on her face.

That last one is worth more to me than all the rest.

Now, the part she asked me to write about. The guide.

A lot of women at her markets ask her how to make the stones. She got tired of explaining it standing up, so she wrote it all down. It's called The Scented Stones Method. The formula, how to keep them from cracking, how to make them white, how to make the scent last, how to package them, how to price them, how to talk to shops, and the messages she sends to customers.

I read it. All of it. I'm a guy who fixes engines, and I can't tell a dried rose from a dried carnation. I could make these from that guide. It's that clear.

And it's dirt cheap, which I insisted on, because I remember what it was like when we couldn't spend money on anything that wasn't groceries.

So here's what I want to say, man to man, if you're a husband who happens to be reading this.

If your wife has been talking about wanting to do something of her own, don't be me at the beginning. Don't say anything about the flour. Buy her the guide. Clear off the kitchen table. Load the car on Saturday.

And if you're the wife, reading this over his shoulder, send him this. Tell him Michael said so.
