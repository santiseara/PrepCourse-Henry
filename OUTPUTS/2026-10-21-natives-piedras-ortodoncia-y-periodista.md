NATIVE 18 | La ortodoncia de Owen
======================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN
Candid iPhone photo, 3:4 vertical, of a white kitchen fridge door in a modest home, slightly off-center, warm morning light from a window on the left. Taped to the fridge with blue painter's tape: a hand-drawn paper "thermometer" chart made by a child with colored markers, the top labeled "OWEN'S TEETH" and a goal amount in big uneven numbers, the thermometer filled in red marker almost to the top, with small handwritten weekly amounts along the side (blurred and illegible). Around it: a school calendar, a pizza coupon magnet, a dentist appointment card, and a small kraft paper bag with two white and dusty pink scented stones with pressed dried flowers peeking out, held by a magnet clip. Slight grain, natural shadows, real lived-in kitchen, no people, no text overlays, not staged.

DESCRIPCIÓN
In August, an orthodontist looked at my son's X-rays, turned his chair toward me, and said, "Three thousand four hundred dollars."

Owen is twelve. His teeth are crowded in a way that's not just cosmetic. They had to be fixed, and our insurance covered exactly nothing, because of course it didn't.

The receptionist explained the options. A payment plan with interest, or pay in full up front and get five percent off. Three thousand two hundred and thirty dollars.

I said, "We'll do the plan," because that's what I always say. Then I sat in the car in the parking lot for a long time.

I'm Emma. I'm forty-two, I live in a small town in Pennsylvania, and at the time I worked part-time at a flower shop. My husband, Michael, is a mechanic. We're fine, mostly. But we're the kind of fine where a three-thousand-dollar bill means the credit card, and the credit card means a year of interest.

For a few months before that, I'd been making little scented stones at the kitchen table. Small, pretty things with pressed flowers and essential oils. I'd sold some to friends, made maybe a couple hundred dollars total, and called it a hobby.

That night, I did the math on the back of the appointment card. Each stone cost me about fifty cents to make. I sold them for twelve. If I could sell around twenty-five a week, I could pay the cash price in about three months.

I told Michael. He said, "That's a lot of rocks." My daughter, Lily, said nothing, went to her room, and came back with a paper thermometer she'd drawn with markers. At the top she wrote "OWEN'S TEETH." She taped it to the fridge.

That's the photo. Here's how it filled up.

Week 1: $96

Eight stones, all to women at the flower shop. I brought a little tray in and left it by the register. I was embarrassed the whole day. Nobody else was.

Week 2: $168

Fourteen stones. Two of the women from week one came back and bought more as gifts. That was the first time I understood that people don't just buy one.

Week 3: $252

I started asking. Not pushing, just asking. "Do you know anyone who'd want some?" My sister-in-law ordered twelve for her book club.

Week 4: $36

Lily got the flu, then I got the flu. I made nothing and sold three. I almost took the thermometer down. Michael wouldn't let me.

Week 5: $312

My first wholesale order. A small gift shop two towns over took thirty stones at six dollars each to sell on their counter. I'd been too scared to ask for weeks. The owner said yes in about forty seconds.

Week 6: $288

The gift shop called. They'd sold half. I also sold a dozen to parents at Owen's soccer practice, which he thought was the most humiliating thing that had ever happened to him.

Week 7: $420

This is my favorite week. At Owen's consultation follow-up, I brought a small box of stones for the receptionist, just as a thank you. She put one on the front desk. The next day, the office manager called and ordered forty for their waiting rooms. The orthodontist's office, buying stones to help pay the orthodontist. I laughed for an hour.

Week 8: $336

The gift shop reordered. I started keeping a little notebook of who bought what and when, so I could text people when their scent started fading after a few weeks.

Week 9: $384

Those texts worked better than anything else I'd tried. Almost everyone said yes to a fresh set.

Week 10: $300

A quiet week. Still more than I made at the flower shop for the same hours.

Week 11: $372

Christmas orders started coming in early. A woman from church ordered twenty for her family.

Week 12: $276

Lily filled in the last bit of red on a Sunday night. Three thousand two hundred and forty dollars. Ten dollars over.

We spent the ten dollars on milkshakes.

The next morning, I walked into the orthodontist's office and paid in full. The same receptionist was at the desk. She'd heard the whole story by then. She came around the counter and hugged me.

I want to be honest about a few things, because I hate it when people aren't.

These weren't easy weeks. I made stones two or three nights a week, after dinner, while the kids did homework. Some nights I was tired and didn't want to. I didn't quit my job. I didn't become a business genius. I just had a number on my fridge, and I kept going.

And I didn't figure it out alone. Back when I started, I bought a short, dirt-cheap guide called The Scented Stones Method. That's where I learned the part that actually matters. How to get the stones smooth and white instead of lumpy and gray, how to keep them from cracking, how to make the scent last, how to package them so they look like they came from a boutique, how to price them, and what to say when you walk into a shop to ask about wholesale. I used that last part in week five, almost word for word.

Owen got his braces in November. He hates them. He'll thank me in about ten years.

The thermometer is still on the fridge. Lily drew a new one next to it. It says "FAMILY TRIP" at the top, and there's already a little red at the bottom.

So here's my question for you.

What's the number on your fridge? The dentist, the car, the credit card, the thing you keep saying you'll deal with later?

Draw it. Tape it up. Then figure out what you can make at your kitchen table that someone will pay for.

NATIVE 19 | "Esto es una estafa": la periodista escéptica
======================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN
Candid iPhone photo, 3:4 vertical, of a cluttered newsroom desk in a small local newspaper office, cool fluorescent light mixed with daylight from a window. On the desk: an open reporter's notebook with handwritten notes and a few lines crossed out (blurred and illegible), a printed receipt next to a small pile of grocery items (a bag of flour, a box of salt, a tiny amber bottle of essential oil), a silicone mold, and a row of six small round homemade scented stones in white and soft sage green, two of them slightly uneven, one with a visible crack. A laptop pushed to the side, a coffee cup with a lid, a press badge on a lanyard lying across the notebook. Slight grain, natural shadows, real messy workspace, no people, no readable text, not staged.

DESCRIPCIÓN
My editor sent me the ad with one line: "Find out if this is a scam."

I've been a reporter at a small-town paper in Pennsylvania for eleven years. A good part of my job is exactly this. A reader sends in something that looks too good to be true, and I find out why.

The ad was for a guide called The Scented Stones Method. A woman named Emma Carter, from a town about forty minutes from mine, claimed she makes little scented stones at her kitchen table for about fifty cents each and sells them for twelve dollars. She said anyone could do it, and the guide was cheap.

I've written about the "work from home" envelope-stuffing jobs. I've written about the leggings company that left a retired teacher with eleven thousand dollars of inventory in her basement. I've seen this movie. I was sure I knew how it ended.

So I bought the guide with my own credit card, and I tested every claim. Here's what I found.

Claim one: "It costs about fifty cents to make each stone."

I went to the grocery store and the craft store and kept every receipt. The basic ingredients came from the baking aisle. The essential oil and a silicone mold were the only real expenses, and both last for dozens of batches. When I divided everything by the number of stones I made over two weeks, I got forty-six cents each.

Verdict: true. If anything, slightly conservative.

Claim two: "You don't need any experience."

My first batch was bad. Two cracked while drying, and the rest came out a sort of dull beige. I'll admit I felt a little vindicated.

Then I went back to the guide and realized I'd skipped a step about drying them slowly. I had also used the wrong kind of flour, which the guide specifically warns about. My second batch came out smooth and white. My third batch, with pressed flowers, looked like something I'd seen at a gift shop in Philadelphia for eighteen dollars.

Verdict: true, if you actually read the instructions. I'm a reporter, not a crafter. I have killed every houseplant I've ever owned.

Claim three: "People will actually pay twelve dollars for them."

This was the claim I was most sure was false. Who pays twelve dollars for a homemade stone?

I took ten to the newsroom and left them on the break room table with a sign: twelve dollars, cash or Venmo. By the end of the day, I'd sold four. Our sports editor, a man who wears the same fleece every day, bought one for his wife.

I took the other six to the coffee shop where I write on weekends and asked the owner if she'd put them by the register. She sold all six by Sunday and asked if I could bring more.

Verdict: true. I was wrong about this one, and I've thought about why. They don't look homemade. They smell good for weeks. And twelve dollars is the exact price of a gift you can buy without thinking about it.

Claim four: "The guide is cheap, with no catch."

This is where these things usually fall apart. The cheap guide is the bait, and then come the upsells, the monthly membership, the coaching calls, the person who won't stop texting you.

I paid once. Nobody called me. Nobody added me to a group. There's no subscription, and I wasn't charged again. The guide itself is short and practical. It covers the recipe, drying, color, making the scent last, packaging, pricing, and how to approach shops about wholesale. That's about it.

Verdict: true. I checked my statement twice.

Claim five: "You can make real money."

This is the one that deserves the most honesty, so here it is.

I drove out to meet Emma Carter. She's forty-two, with a husband who's a mechanic, two kids, and a dog that sat on my feet the entire interview. She showed me her notebook: what she spent, what she sold, every week, going back months. She sells to customers, at a few local markets, and to shops that buy at six dollars a stone and reorder. In a good month, it's a real second income. In a slow month, it's grocery money.

She works two or three evenings a week. She isn't rich. She told me that herself, before I could ask.

Verdict: true, with the honest version attached. This is not a get-rich-quick scheme. It's a small product with a big margin, and it pays you exactly as much as you put into making and selling it.

When I got back to the office, my editor asked, "So? Scam?"

I said, "No. It's work. Small, cheap, honest work. Which is the opposite of a scam."

He told me to write it up. This is the write-up.

I'll add one personal note, which a reporter isn't really supposed to do.

I kept making them. Not a lot. A batch every other weekend. The coffee shop takes twenty a month now. It's paying for my car insurance, which I find hilarious, given how this started.

If you've been burned before, and a lot of us have, I understand being skeptical. I was. So don't take my word for it, or Emma's. Run the same test I did. Buy the guide, keep your receipts, make a batch, and put ten on a table at work.
