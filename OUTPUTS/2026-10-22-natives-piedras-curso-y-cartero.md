NATIVE 20 | Los $2.000 que casi gasto en un curso

DESCRIPCIÓN
At 1:14 in the morning, on a Tuesday in March, I had my credit card number typed into a checkout page for a course that cost nineteen hundred and ninety-seven dollars.

I want to tell you how I got there, and what stopped me, because I think a lot of women are sitting where I was sitting that night.

I'm Emma. I'm forty-two, I live in a small town in Pennsylvania, and back then I worked part-time at a flower shop for thirteen dollars an hour. My husband, Michael, is a mechanic. That winter, our furnace died, our car needed a transmission, and I was quietly moving bills from one month to the next and hoping nobody noticed.

I'd been watching videos about making money online for weeks. You've probably seen the same ones. A woman in a bright kitchen saying she replaced her husband's income in six months. Then a free training. Then another free training. Then a phone call with a "success coach" who was very warm and very interested in how much was on my credit card.

The course was eight weeks of videos. A "mindset" module. A private community. Weekly group calls. It would teach me to build an online business selling to strangers on the internet. There was a payment plan. Six payments of three hundred and forty-seven dollars. The coach told me that if I didn't invest in myself, nobody would.

So there I was at 1:14 in the morning, with my card typed in and my thumb on the trackpad.

And my daughter, Lily, walked into the kitchen for a glass of water.

She looked at the screen, and she looked at me, and she said, "Mom, what is that?"

I didn't have a good answer. I tried to explain it, and I heard myself saying words like "funnel" and "scaling" that I didn't really understand. Lily is nine. She said, "But what do you sell?"

I closed the laptop.

The next week, at the flower shop, a customer came in with a little box of scented stones she'd bought at a craft fair. They were beautiful. Smooth, white, with dried flowers pressed in, and they smelled like eucalyptus. She paid twelve dollars for one. When she left, my boss said, "Those are just a few pantry ingredients and a mold, you know." She said it like it was a bad thing. I didn't hear it as a bad thing.

That night, I found a short guide called The Scented Stones Method. It was dirt cheap. Less than I'd spend on a pizza for the family. I bought it, and I bought about fifteen dollars of supplies.

Here's the honest comparison, because I've thought about it a lot.

What the $1,997 course promised: an online business, someday, after eight weeks of lessons, if I built a website, grew an audience and ran ads.

What the cheap guide actually did: showed me how to make one product, how to make it look like it came from a boutique, how to price it, and who to sell it to. People I already knew, and the shops in my own town.

What the course said I'd need: a laptop, software subscriptions, an ad budget and "a strong mindset."

What I actually needed: my kitchen table, two evenings a week, and fifteen dollars.

How long the course said it would take to see results: "Most students see momentum in three to six months."

How long it actually took me: nine days. That's when I sold my first stone, to a coworker, for twelve dollars. Each one cost me about fifty cents to make.

By June, I had two gift shops buying twenty stones at a time, at six dollars each. By the fall, I'd paid off the transmission. I never put a single dollar of that business on a credit card.

I don't know what would have happened if I'd bought that course. Maybe it would have worked for me. I know it works for some people. But I know what my credit card statement would have looked like for six months, and I know I would have spent the summer watching videos instead of selling.

So here's what I want to leave you with. These are the four questions I ask now, before I spend money on anything that promises to help me make money.

One. Can I explain what I'm going to sell, in one sentence, to a nine-year-old? If I can't, I don't understand it yet.

Two. Who is going to pay me, and do I already know some of them? If the answer is "strangers on the internet, eventually," that's a long road.

Three. How fast can I make back what I'm spending? If it's months, I need to be able to afford those months.

Four. Is someone pressuring me to decide tonight? A good opportunity will still be there in the morning.

The Scented Stones Method passed all four. Lily could explain my business now in one sentence: "Mom makes pretty rocks that smell nice and people buy them." I made the guide back on my first sale. And nobody ever called me.

If you're sitting where I was, with a cart open and a knot in your stomach, close the laptop for one night. Ask yourself those four questions.

DESCRIPCIÓN
My husband, Ray, has been a rural mail carrier for twenty-two years. He knows every mailbox, every dog, and every person on a ninety-mile route through the farm country of central Pennsylvania.

So when he came home one night last spring and said, "There's a woman on my route who ships more boxes than the hardware store," I paid attention.

My name is Denise. I'm fifty-one. That spring, the school district cut the cafeteria staff, and after fourteen years of serving lunch to kids I'd watched grow up, I was out of a job.

Where we live, there aren't many jobs to begin with. The nearest Walmart is forty minutes away. Most of what's left is seasonal or part-time, and I'd already applied to all of it.

Ray told me about this woman because he was curious, not because he thought it had anything to do with me. Every week, more little boxes. Gift shops two counties over. Addresses in Ohio and New Jersey. So one day, when she came out to hand him a stack of them, he finally asked.

"What are you selling in there?"

She laughed and showed him. Scented stones. Little white ones with pressed flowers, that smelled like lavender. She said she made them at her kitchen table, for about fifty cents each, and sold them for twelve dollars. Shops bought them wholesale at six.

Her name was Emma. She told Ray the guide she'd learned from was cheap, and he wrote the name on the back of a parcel slip. It was The Scented Stones Method.

He handed me that slip at dinner and said, "I don't know. Maybe."

I almost threw it out. I'm not a crafty person. And I figured that kind of thing only works if you live in a town with boutiques and coffee shops and people with money to spend on pretty things. Not out here, where the closest thing to a boutique is the feed store.

But I didn't have anything else, so I bought it.

The first batch took me an evening. The second came out better. By the end of the week, I had thirty stones lined up on wax paper on my dining room table, and no idea who to sell them to.

That's when Ray had the idea.

His route goes past seven farm stands. You know the kind. A wooden shelf by the road with pumpkins, eggs, honey, and a metal box with a slot for cash. Nobody stands there. People pull over, take what they want, and leave the money. Out here, it works. It's worked for a hundred years.

Ray knew every family who ran those stands. So on a Saturday, I rode along with him in the truck, and at each one I asked the same thing. "Can I put a little tray of these on your stand? You keep two dollars from every one."

Five of the seven said yes. One of them, an older woman named Ruth, smelled one and said, "Oh, honey, you should be charging more."

I put them out on a Monday at twelve dollars each.

Ray checked them on his route every day, which I thought was very romantic, and he told me that was ridiculous. On Wednesday he came home and said, "Ruth's stand is empty." By Friday, three more were.

That first week, I sold thirty-one stones. That was three hundred and seventy-two dollars, minus sixty-two for the farm families. About three hundred and ten dollars for me, from stones that cost me fifteen dollars to make.

I sat in the truck in our driveway and cried.

Here's what I've figured out since then, for anyone who lives somewhere like I do.

People out here aren't poor in the way city people think. They just don't have anywhere to spend money on small, nice things. A twelve-dollar gift on the side of the road, that smells like fall, is exactly what they want to bring home.

Farm stands are the best store you'll ever have. No rent, no staff, and the owners are happy to make a few dollars for doing nothing.

And the honesty box works. In six months, I've had exactly one stone go missing, and the next week someone left an extra twenty with a note that said "for the one my grandson took."

By the summer, I was on eleven stands. The church started asking for them for the fall bazaar. The diner by the post office keeps a basket on the counter now, and the waitress tells everyone who made them.

It's not a fortune. Most months, it's somewhere between six and nine hundred dollars. But that's about what the school paid me, and I make them in my own dining room, with the radio on, three evenings a week.

Ray still delivers Emma's boxes. He delivers some of mine now too, to people who bought one on a farm stand and wanted more for Christmas.

He says he's the only mailman in Pennsylvania who's in the scented stone business twice.

If you live somewhere small, and you've been telling yourself there's no money to be made where you are, I was telling myself the same thing. Look at the roads you already drive.
