NATIVE 1 - THE ELEVEN PAGES TAPED INSIDE MY STATION DRAWER
(angulo permiso / la carpetita - modela el CJTO 6, mejor CPA con volumen)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, shot from above by the stylist standing at her own station. An open hairdresser's station drawer. Taped to the inside of the drawer lid: eleven creased, laminated paper pages, overlapping each other, each one showing a simple hand-drawn line diagram of a woman's head from the side with sectioning lines, an angle marked with a protractor curve, and hand-numbered steps 1 to 8 in blue pen. The pages are curled at the corners, one has a round coffee stain, one is held on with yellowed tape, one has been written over twice. In the drawer below: cutting shears, a carbon comb with one broken tooth, sectioning clips, a roll of neck strips, hair clippings in the corner of the drawer. Cheap overhead salon fluorescent light, slight glare on the laminate, the shadow of the phone across the bottom corner. No people, no faces, no brand names. Handwritten numbers only. Shot on an older iPhone, slightly soft, real small-town salon, not a studio, not styled.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There are eleven pages taped inside the door of my station drawer. I have been cutting hair for twenty-nine years and I still open that drawer before certain cuts. Let me tell you what is on those pages and why I stopped being embarrassed about them.

My name is Rhonda Teague. I am fifty-four. I have been a licensed cosmetologist since 1997 and I have rented chair two at the same salon in Fort Wayne, Indiana, for the last sixteen years.

I am good at my job. I need you to know that before I tell you the rest, because what I am about to say gets taken the wrong way.

I can cut a one-length bob with my eyes half closed. I have done the same four women every six weeks for a decade. I know how Carol's crown grows, I know Jeanine's cowlick, I know that Maureen's hair looks two inches longer the second it dries.

And there are cuts I have been quietly steering women away from for twenty-nine years.

Not because I do not know how. Because I do not know how right now, at ten in the morning, with her sitting there looking at me in the mirror, waiting.

Those are two completely different things and nobody in this industry ever says it out loud.

Here is what it looks like from behind the chair.

She sits down. She is happy. She has her phone out and she turns it around to show me a photo. A butterfly cut, or a short shag, or an angled bob with a longer front.

I recognize it instantly. I know exactly what it is. I have seen that cut four hundred times.

And in the three seconds it takes me to reach for my comb, my brain tries to pull up the one thing I need and comes back with nothing.

Where does the first section go. What am I holding my guide at. Am I over-directing the front or keeping it natural. Where is the corner.

The information is in there somewhere. I learned it. I have done it. I just cannot get it out of my head in the four seconds I have before I have to say something to her.

So I say the thing.

"Hmm, with your texture I would maybe go a little softer than that."

She says okay, Rhonda, whatever you think.

And I give her a version of the cut she asked for. A safe one. The one I can do without the floor dropping out from under me.

She leaves happy. She always leaves happy. That is not the part that bothers me.

What bothers me is the ten minutes after she leaves, when I am sweeping up and I know exactly what I did.

Let me tell you about Bridget.

Bridget Coyne. She has been in my chair since 2011. Dental hygienist, two boys, drives in from Huntertown every five weeks, always the eleven o'clock on a Thursday, always brings me the Girl Scout cookies her niece is selling whether I want them or not.

Two Marches ago Bridget sat down with her phone and asked me for a shag. Not a trim. A proper modern shag, with the short curtain pieces at the front.

I looked at it and my stomach did the thing.

I said the thing. Soft layers, keep the length, let's not go too far all at once.

She said okay, Rhonda, whatever you think.

Six weeks later she did not book. Seven weeks. Eight.

I saw it in October on Facebook. She was at somebody's wedding in a dress I had never seen her in, and she had the shag. The real one. Curtain pieces at the front and everything. She looked twelve years younger and she looked like herself.

Somebody else cut it. Somebody with less experience than me, in a salon six minutes from mine.

Bridget never said a word to me. She never complained. She never asked me to fix anything. She just quietly went somewhere that would give her what she asked for.

I want to be clear about something. That was not a skill problem. If you had put me in a classroom that day with a mannequin head and a printed diagram, I would have cut that shag perfectly.

It was a recall problem. In the four seconds I had, standing behind her, I could not get the sectioning out of my own head.

That is the whole thing. That is what I had been calling a confidence problem for twenty-nine years. It is not confidence. It is not technique. It is memory under pressure, in front of a person who is watching your face in a mirror.

And memory under pressure is not something you fix by learning more.

I tried learning more. I want you to know I tried.

I bought a four-hundred-dollar online cutting course in 2019. Fourteen modules. I watched three. Not because it was bad, because it was eleven minutes of talking before the first section, and my day does not have eleven minutes in it.

I have watched tutorials on my phone in the back room with the water running so nobody could hear it. I have screenshotted somebody's hands on Instagram and then not been able to find the screenshot three weeks later when I actually needed it. I have asked in a stylist Facebook group and gotten forty answers that contradicted each other and two people arguing about elevation in the comments.

None of it is there in the four seconds. That is the only moment that matters, and none of it is there.

So about seven years ago I started making my own pages.

Every time I got through a cut I do not do often, I would sit in my chair after close with a sheet of paper and draw the head and write down what I actually did. Where the sections went. What I held it at. Where the guide came from. What I would do differently.

Then I laminated them at the Office Max on Coldwater Road and taped them inside my drawer.

Eleven pages. That is twenty-nine years of hair, and I got eleven pages out of it.

They work. That is the maddening part. The cuts on those eleven pages are the cuts I never hesitate on anymore. I open the drawer, I look for about eight seconds, I close it, and my hands know what to do.

But eleven pages is not a career. There are two hundred cuts out there and I have eleven.

Last spring the girl who rents chair four, Alyssa, who is twenty-six and has been licensed for four years, watched me open my drawer before a pixie.

I got defensive. I said something stupid like "I know, I know, the old lady and her papers."

She said, "Rhonda, why is yours on paper?"

She turned her phone around. She had the same thing I had, except it was not eleven pages, it was a whole book of them. One page per cut. Side view of the final shape, the sectioning pattern drawn right on the head, the elevation, where the guide comes from, and the order to do it in, numbered.

She said she keeps it open on her phone on the shelf under the mirror. She checks it for twenty seconds before she starts. Sometimes she checks it again in the middle.

I asked her if she felt like she was cheating.

She looked at me like I had asked if breathing was cheating. She said, "It is a reference. Surgeons have references."

I am fifty-four years old and a twenty-six-year-old had to tell me that.

It is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. One page per cut. Twenty-five of them. It is not a course. There is nothing to finish, nothing to log into, no modules, nobody talking at me for eleven minutes.

Here is the honest part, and then I am done.

The first cut I used it on, it told me not to do what I was about to do. I had a long bob with a longer front and I was going to take the back up to forty-five degrees the way I always do, out of habit. The page had it at zero with a diagonal forward line and the guide traveling from the nape. I stood there and argued with a piece of paper in my own head for about five seconds and then I did it the way the page said.

It sat better than any of mine ever had. That cut was two inches different from how I have been doing it for eleven years.

The second one was a hush cut, which I had turned down twice. Thirty seconds of looking before I started. One more look when I got to the front. The woman cried in my chair, the good way, and she has been back twice.

That is it. That is my whole story. Two cuts, one no and one yes.

What I needed, and what I never had until this year, is a reference that works in the only moment that counts:

One page per cut. Not a video I have to scrub through with scissors in my hand.
Sectioning, elevation and guide on the same page as the picture of the final shape. Those three things or it is just a hairstyle photo, and I do not need more hairstyle photos.
An order to follow. Numbered. First this section, then this one.
Readable in under a minute, on a phone, propped against a mirror, with wet hands.
The mistake written down next to the step where people make it.

It does all five. It is dirt cheap, which still bothers me a little given what that course cost me in 2019.
Bridget booked with me again in July. Her sister told her I do the shag now. I do not know if that is true or if her sister made it up, but she sat down and asked and this time I said yes and then I checked the page for thirty seconds before I picked up my shears.

She did not notice. Nobody ever notices.

There are still eleven pages taped inside my drawer. I have not taken them down. I am sentimental about them. But I do not open the drawer anymore.

Rhonda T.
Licensed cosmetologist, 29 years
Fort Wayne, Indiana

P.S. If you are reading this thinking "I have been doing hair for twenty-five years, I should not need this" - that was my exact sentence, for about seven years, while I quietly talked women out of the cuts they came in for. Nobody is grading you on how much you can hold in your head at ten in the morning. The woman in your chair is not grading you on that either. She just wants the cut she asked for.
P.P.S. On the phone thing. I worried about this more than anything else, so here is what actually happens: I look at it while she is still in the shampoo chair, or while I am sectioning, which is the exact moment every stylist alive is looking down anyway. It is twenty to thirty seconds. In two years nobody has asked me what I am looking at. Alyssa has been doing it in front of her clients for four years. Clients do not see a stylist checking a reference. They see a stylist taking a second before she starts, which is the most reassuring thing you can do in front of a mirror.
P.P.P.S. It is not Pinterest photos. I want to be blunt about that because that is what I assumed. Pinterest is the result. Anybody can see the result. What I never had was the back of the head with the sectioning drawn on it and the degree I am supposed to hold it at, which is the entire job. If it had been photos of finished haircuts I would have wanted my money back in ten minutes.
P.P.P.P.S. I printed mine. I know that is not the point of it, but I am fifty-four and I like paper. It works either way, which is the only reason I am mentioning it. The pages that are taped in my drawer are now the ones I printed out of it, and the eleven old ones are in a folder in my tote bag because I cannot throw them out.

NATIVE 2 - THE TWO PILES OF HAIR ON MY FLOOR WERE NOT THE SAME SIZE
(angulo el lado distinto / la correccion - modela el CJTO 2)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist looking down at her own salon floor at the end of the day. On worn grey linoleum around the base of a hydraulic chair: two separate piles of freshly cut brown hair, clearly different sizes, one noticeably bigger than the other, swept apart from each other. A broom leaning against the chair, a dustpan on the floor, a few clips and a neck strip dropped on the ground, one sectioning clip still holding a small lock of hair. Late afternoon light coming in low from a storefront window on the left, long shadows, the salon empty behind. No people, no faces, no text, no brand names. Shot on a phone, slightly tilted, grainy, real working salon floor with scuff marks, not staged.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

I swept up after a long bob last February and the two piles of hair on my floor were not the same size. Nobody saw it but me. I have been doing this for twenty-five years and I want to tell you what that pile actually meant, because I spent two decades getting it wrong.

My name is Gina Petrosky. I am forty-nine. I have been licensed since 2001 and I own a two-chair shop on a corner in Erie, Pennsylvania, between a tax place and a pizza shop that has changed its name four times.

The cut was fine. I want to say that first. She looked good, she tipped me, she took a picture in the parking lot.

But I knew before she was out the door. I had evened the right side twice. Then I had taken a little off the left to match. Then a little more off the right. By the end I was three quarters of an inch shorter than where I started and I had chased that line around her head like a dog chasing a car.

And the floor told on me. Two piles. One from the cut, one from the chasing.

If you have never done this, this ad is not for you. If you just felt something in your stomach, keep reading, because it took me twenty-five years to find out that it is not what you think it is.

Here is what the chasing actually is.

It is not bad hands. My hands are fine. My hands have been doing this since the Clinton administration.

It is not bad scissors. It is not her hair "being difficult." It is not the chair being crooked, although I have blamed the chair.

It is that somewhere in the first four minutes, before any of it was visible, I did not have a guide I could trust. I sectioned by feel because I was talking to her about her daughter's wedding, I set a guide that was a little long on one side, and then I spent thirty-five minutes trying to fix a mistake with a tool that cannot fix it. Scissors cannot fix a guide. Scissors can only take more.

The cut is decided in the first four minutes. Everything after that is either executing or chasing.

I did not learn that in school. I learned it at forty-eight years old from a diagram.

But first let me tell you about Denise, because Denise is the reason I am writing this at all.

Denise Hollenbeck sat in my chair for sixteen years. Sixteen. She was with me when I rented the chair and she came with me when I bought the shop. She is a court reporter, she is funny, she talks the entire time and I never minded once.

In 2023 she started asking me for a graduated bob with some weight in the back. I did it. It was fine. Six weeks later, same thing. Fine again.

Then she started saying the sentence. Not complaining. Just saying it, in the mirror, with a little laugh.

"Is this side a little longer, or is it me?"

It was not her. It was a little longer. And it was a little longer because I was setting my guide at the back by eye and then over-directing one side more than the other without knowing I was doing it, every single time, for a year and a half.

She stopped saying it eventually. Then she started coming every ten weeks instead of six. Then she stopped.

She did not fire me. People do not fire their hairdresser of sixteen years. They just get busy. That is how it goes. She is still in my Christmas card list and I am still in hers and we have not been in a room together in two years.

I had to look at that for a long time before I would admit what it was. I thought I was tired. I thought maybe I had lost a step. I am forty-nine, that is what you tell yourself.

I had not lost a step. I was doing a technical thing wrong in a place I could not see, at a moment when nothing looked wrong yet, in exactly the same way, over and over.

You cannot correct something you cannot see. And you cannot see it in the mirror, because in the mirror everything is already wet and already pulled and already fine.

Here is everything I tried.

I bought better shears. Twice. Beautiful shears. The problem was not the shears.

I started telling clients "wet hair lies" and making them come back in a week for a free check, which is a nice thing to do for people and also a way of letting someone else's eyes catch what mine were missing.

I watched videos. You cannot watch a video during a haircut. Let us stop pretending. You have scissors in one hand, a comb in your mouth, a woman in the chair and a phone that locks itself every thirty seconds. By the time I scrubbed back to the part I needed, I had lost the section I was holding.

I went to a two-day class in Pittsburgh in 2019 that I paid for myself and loved and remembered almost none of by the following Tuesday.

What finally changed it was the girl who rents my second chair. Shelby. She is thirty-one. She came out of school with less than half my hours and she was doing cuts I would not touch, and I had quietly decided she was just one of those naturally gifted ones.

She is not. I asked her. She laughed at me.

She said she checks the sectioning before every cut she has not done in a while. Twenty seconds on her phone, propped on the shelf, before the first cut, while the client is still settling in.

She showed me the page for a graduated bob. Denise's cut. The one I had been doing wrong for a year and a half.

The page had the head drawn from the back with the sectioning pattern on it, the elevation written down, where the guide comes from, and eight numbered steps. And down at the bottom, in a little box, it had the mistake. In writing. The exact thing I had been doing: over-directing one side and letting the guide travel unevenly, which makes one side read longer when it dries even though it measured even when it was wet.

Somebody wrote my mistake down in a box on a page and I had spent a year and a half thinking I was getting old.

I am not going to tell you that fixed everything or that I am a different stylist now. I am the same stylist. I have the same hands and the same shop and the same pizza place next door.

What is different is that I do not chase anymore.

First cut after that, an A-line bob. I read the page for about forty seconds while she was in the shampoo chair. I sectioned the way it said, which was not the way I had been doing it. I set the guide where it said, which felt too conservative, and I almost went back to my way.

I swept up afterward and there was one pile.

That is the whole feeling. One pile. Twenty-five years and the thing that got me was a diagram with a box in the corner telling me what I was about to do wrong.

The thing is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. Twenty-five cuts, one page each. It is not a course and I would not have bought a course. I have paid for two courses in my life and watched about a fifth of them, and I am not going to pretend about that anymore at forty-nine years old.

What a reference has to do, for anyone who is standing where I was standing:

Show the sectioning on the head, from the angle you need to see it from. The back of the head. Not a photo of a finished haircut from the front.
Tell you the elevation and where the guide comes from. Those are the two things that decide whether you execute or chase.
Give you an order. Numbered, so you can find your place if you get interrupted, and you will get interrupted.
Write down the mistake next to the step where it happens. Not a list of general tips at the end. Next to the step.
Open in one second on a phone with a wet hand, or print, because half of us still like paper.

It does those five things and nothing else, which is why it is worth having. And it is dirt cheap, which annoys me when I think about Pittsburgh.
I am not going to tell you Denise is coming back. She is not. Sixteen years ended because of a guide I set by eye while we were talking about her daughter's wedding.

But there is a woman named Arlene who comes in on Fridays who asked me three weeks ago whether I had gotten new scissors, because something was different.

Same scissors. One pile on the floor.

Gina P.
Licensed cosmetologist, 25 years
Erie, Pennsylvania

P.S. I did not have a technique problem and if that is what you heard, read it again. I can execute anything you put in front of me. What I did not have was the thing you need in the four minutes before any of it is visible, written down somewhere I could reach it with a client in the chair. There is a difference between not knowing how and not having it in front of you, and the whole industry acts like only the first one exists.
P.P.S. If you are the one who has been quietly getting checked by somebody else - a coworker, your daughter, the owner walking past - you already know this is not about hands. Having another set of eyes is a workaround for not having the information. It works, and it also means you are depending on who happens to be working that day.
P.P.P.S. For the women who are self-taught, or who did school twenty years ago in another state, or who learned in somebody's kitchen: you are exactly who this is easiest for. You have the hands already. What you were never handed was the sectioning on paper. I went to a real school and graduated with honors and I still had to learn the graduated bob properly at forty-eight from a diagram, so nobody here is behind anybody.

NATIVE 3 - THE GIRL AT THE NEXT CHAIR TOOK THE CUT I TURNED DOWN
(angulo el tercero mas joven / el menu que se encoge)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist standing at her station looking down at the shelf under her mirror. A phone propped upright against the mirror base showing a clean printed reference page: a line drawing of a woman's head seen from the back with sectioning lines drawn across it, a small angle diagram, and short numbered steps down the right side. In front of the phone on the shelf: open cutting shears, a carbon comb, three sectioning clips, a water bottle with a cracked trigger, a coffee mug with lipstick on the rim. Reflection of the empty salon in the bottom of the mirror, chairs behind. Warm salon light from above, slight glare on the phone screen, phone shadow on the shelf. No people, no faces, no brand names, no readable words other than the step numbers. Shot on a phone, handheld, real working station, cluttered, not staged.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

A twenty-four-year-old at the next chair took the haircut I turned down, and I stood there and watched her look at her phone for twenty seconds before she started. I am fifty-eight and I have been licensed for thirty-one years. That twenty seconds is the whole reason I am writing this.

My name is Lorraine Vasquez. I have been a cosmetologist since 1995. I have worked in six salons, owned one that did not make it, and for the last nine years I have been at a salon on Speedway in Tucson, Arizona, with my own station and my own key.

I am the one the other girls come to when a client is upset. I am the one who knows how to talk somebody down from a bad box dye. Thirty-one years buys you that.

And over those thirty-one years, without ever deciding to, I let my menu shrink.

Not my prices. My menu. The list of cuts I will actually say yes to.

When I was twenty-nine I would try anything. By fifty-five I was doing four cuts. A one-length, a long layer with some face framing, a classic bob, and a short layered thing for the older ladies that I could do in my sleep. Everything else, I had a way of handling.

"That is going to be really high maintenance for you."
"With your density I would not go that short."
"Let's do a version of that."

Every one of those sentences is true enough to say with a straight face. That is what makes them so easy to say. I said them for years and I never once wrote down what they actually were.

They were me declining.

Here is the day it stopped being invisible to me.

It was a Tuesday in April. A woman came in, maybe thirty-five, teacher at a charter school, and she had a photo of a butterfly cut. Long on the bottom, short layers up top, the whole thing moving.

I know what a butterfly cut is. I have watched it come and go. It is layers and over-direction, that is all it is, and in 1998 I was cutting things far harder than that out of my head.

But I looked at that photo and I could not get the sectioning to come up. Where is the top section ending. Where am I setting the short guide. How far back am I over-directing it.

Nothing. Thirty-one years of hair and nothing.

So I said, "You know what, with your fine hair, I would be nervous about how thin the ends get. Let me do you a really pretty long layer instead."

She said okay. They always say okay. That is the part that should scare all of us.

And Nicolette, who is twenty-four and rents the chair next to mine and has been licensed for two and a half years, heard the whole thing.

Later that week the same woman came back, booked with Nicolette, with the same photo.

I watched it happen from six feet away.

Nicolette sat her down. Then she picked up her phone, propped it against the mirror, and looked at it for about twenty seconds. Not scrolling. Reading one page. Then she put her hands in that woman's hair and cut a butterfly, start to finish, and it was good. It was better than good.

She looked at the page one more time when she got to the front sections.

I went in the back room and I was angrier than I have been in a decade, and about four minutes into being angry I realized who I was angry at.

Not at her. Not at the client. At the twenty-nine years I spent believing that looking something up meant I did not know it.

That is the belief. That is the whole thing. Somewhere in cosmetology school in 1994 somebody got it into my head that a real professional has it all in her hands, and if you have to check, you are not there yet.

So I did not check. I declined instead. For years. And declining looks like professional judgment, which is what makes it so comfortable.

I asked Nicolette about it the next morning before we opened. I was embarrassed, so I made it sound casual.

She said, and I am going to quote her because I have thought about it every week since: "Lorraine, I know how to do it. I just do not know it by heart. Those are not the same thing."

Thirty-one years. Nobody had ever said that sentence to me.

She showed me what was on her phone. One page per haircut. The final shape drawn from the side. The sectioning pattern drawn right on the head, from the back and the top, where you actually need to see it. The elevation. Where the guide comes from. Eight numbered steps. A short box of the mistakes people make on that specific cut, and a line at the bottom for what to check before the cape comes off.

Not a video. Not a course. Not somebody talking. A page.

I want to be honest about what I thought when I first looked at it. I thought: this is basic. I know most of this.

And that was exactly right, and it was the point, and it took me a week to understand it. I do know most of it. Knowing it was never the problem. Getting it out of my head at ten in the morning with a teacher in my chair holding up a phone, that was the problem, and that is a completely different problem with a completely different fix.

The first time I used it was on a cut I had been turning down for probably four years. A short shag on a woman with thick, coarse hair. I read the page twice. Once the night before, which I now think is unnecessary, and once for about thirty seconds while she was in the shampoo bowl.

I cut it. It took me longer than it will next time. She has rebooked twice and she brought her sister.

The second time, it told me no. That matters more than the first one, so I will tell you about it.

A woman wanted a pixie with a longer fringe and I was going to start where I always start, at the nape, because that is how I start short cuts. The page had the first section at the top with the guide traveling forward, because the whole shape of that cut depends on the length you set on top, not at the nape. If I had started where I was going to start, I would have been locked into a shape I did not want by the time I got to the top.

I stood there and I had a little argument with a diagram and the diagram was right.

That is two cuts. One no and one yes. That is all the proof I have and I am not going to dress it up.

What is different now is my menu. I said yes to eleven things last month that I would have had a sentence ready for in March.

The thing is The Women's Cutting Atlas. Twenty-five cuts, one page each, plus the sectioning and the angles at the front of it.

If you are where I was, here is what a reference actually has to do, and I would hold anything you are considering against this list:

One page per cut. If I have to scrub through a video with shears in my hand, it does not exist.
The sectioning drawn on the head, from the back and the top. Not a photo of the front of a finished haircut. The front is the result. The back is the job.
Elevation and guide written down, not implied.
Numbered steps, so when you get interrupted - and in a salon you will get interrupted - you can find your place.
The mistake written next to the step where people make it, not in a general tips section.
Readable in under a minute. Phone or printed.

It does all of that and it is dirt cheap, which is its own small insult to the money I have spent on education over thirty-one years.
The teacher with the butterfly cut comes to me now. Nicolette moved to Phoenix in September and left me her clients, which is the nicest thing anybody in this industry has ever done for me, and when that woman sat down I told her the truth. I said I turned her down in April because I could not remember the sectioning, and I can now, because I look it up.

She said, "I thought you just did not like the haircut."

Thirty-one years of professional judgment, and that is what she heard. She thought I did not like it.

Lorraine V.
Licensed cosmetologist, 31 years
Tucson, Arizona

P.S. If the word "look it up" is the part catching in your throat, I understand, because it caught in mine for twenty-nine years. Here is the only thing that moved me: the client does not want a stylist who has it all memorized. She has no idea what you have memorized. She wants the haircut she asked for. The memorizing was never for her. It was for us, and it cost me a decade of my menu.
P.P.S. On being seen with your phone. I thought about this for a week before I tried it and it turned out to be nothing. I read it while she is at the shampoo bowl or while I am sectioning, which is already the moment you are looking down. It is twenty or thirty seconds. Nobody has ever asked. The one time a client did glance over, she said "oh, is that her cut?" and I said yes, and she thought it was wonderful that I was checking.
