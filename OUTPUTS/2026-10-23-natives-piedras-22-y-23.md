NATIVE 22 - THE NIGHTSTAND DRAWER

IMAGE PROMPT (3:4 vertical, phone photo, no faces)
An open nightstand drawer photographed from directly above at night, lit only by a warm bedside lamp just out of
frame. Inside the drawer: a small white scented stone, clearly old, one chipped edge, with a single pressed flower
on top that has faded to brown, resting on a folded tissue. Beside it, a pair of reading glasses, a rosary, a
pharmacy pill organizer and a church bulletin. A woman's hand, late fifties, no face, no jewelry except a thin
wedding band, is lifting the stone out with two fingers. Shallow depth of field, slightly soft focus, visible
grain, the imperfect framing of a snapshot taken quickly with one hand. No text, no logos, no studio lighting.

DESCRIPTION

I was helping my neighbor pack up a house she'd lived in for thirty-one years, and I found something in her
nightstand drawer that I had to go sit in my car about.

My name is Cheryl. I'm 57. I live in Zanesville, Ohio, in the same split-level my husband Dale and I bought in
1994, and I work four days a week behind the counter at the pharmacy on Maple. Dale drives a propane truck. Our
daughter Kayla is 31, lives in Columbus, teaches third grade, and calls me on her drive home on Thursdays. That's
the whole picture. That's been the whole picture for a long time, and I'm not complaining about it.

Judy moved in across the street the same summer we did. Her kids and my kid rode the same bus. For thirty-one
years she was the person I waved to with a coffee cup in my hand, and for the last four, after her Harold passed,
she was the person I checked the porch light for.

In March she fell in her own kitchen. Nothing broke, but her daughter Renee flew in from Tempe and that was that.
By April there was a realtor's sign in the yard and we were putting Judy's whole life into boxes so she could go
live in a casita behind Renee's house in Arizona.

If you have ever packed up a house for somebody, you know the kitchen is nothing. The kitchen is dishes. It's the
drawers that take you down.

We got to the bedroom on the second Saturday. Renee was doing the closet and I was doing the nightstand, and I
opened the top drawer, and sitting in there on a folded tissue, next to her reading glasses and her rosary, was
one of my stones.

I need to back up and tell you what I make, or you won't understand why I had to go sit down.

Eleven years ago I started making little scented stones at my kitchen table. White, about the size of a bar of
soap, with a dried flower pressed into the top. You keep one in a drawer or a closet or your car and it makes the
whole space smell like a garden. My grandmother made something like them. I started because I needed something to
do with my hands at night after Kayla left for college and the house got loud with quiet.

And in eleven years, I have given away every single one I ever made. Every teacher Kayla ever had. Every nurse at
my mother's old doctor's office. Hostess gifts, Christmas, thank-yous, sorry-for-your-losses. I have never sold
one in my life.

The one in Judy's drawer, I knew exactly which one it was. I made it the winter her Harold got his diagnosis,
which was six years ago in February. Lavender. The flower on top had gone brown years back. There was a chip out
of one corner. It had clearly been handled a thousand times.

She'd kept it in the drawer next to her bed for six years. The drawer where you keep what you reach for.

I sat on the edge of Judy's bare mattress holding it, and Renee came in and asked what was wrong, and I said
nothing, and then I had to go out to the driveway for a minute.

Here's the part I'm embarrassed about.

I thought those stones were a nothing. A little something to hand somebody so you're not showing up empty-handed.
That is genuinely what I thought I was giving people for eleven years.

Four years ago I did try to sell them. Once. The church had a rummage sale and I put six on a card table with a
piece of paper that said $3 EACH in marker. I sat there for five hours. I sold one, to a woman I'm fairly sure was
being nice to me. I packed up the other five, drove home, and decided that was my answer. Nobody pays for these.
They're homemade.

And I couldn't ever get them right anyway, is the other thing. I'd learned off a video, flour and salt and warm
water. Half my batches cracked while they dried. The flowers I pressed on fresh went brown in a month. The smell
was strong for about four days and then it was just a rock. I asked in a crafting group on Facebook once, why do
mine crack, and got thirty-one answers, and no two of them agreed, and one woman told me to pray over them. I
figured the difference between mine and the ones in a boutique was something I didn't have. Talent, probably. A
good eye.

So. Judy's drawer.

When I came back in, Renee was sitting on the floor with the stone in her hand, turning it over. Renee is 34 and
sells skincare at a counter in a mall in Tempe and is the most direct person I have ever met.

She said, "Do you know she took this to the hospital with her?"

I did not know that.

She said her mother kept it in her purse the whole eleven days Harold was in Genesis. That a nurse asked about it.
That Judy told the nurse her neighbor made it.

Then Renee said the thing that I have thought about every day since.

She said, "Cheryl. For six years you've been giving away the only thing in this house nobody threw out. And you
charge three dollars for it."

I started to say they're just homemade and she stopped me.

She said, "There's a word for a thing somebody keeps by their bed for six years, and it isn't homemade."

She told me that in Tempe there's a woman at her farmers market selling the same idea, worse flowers, for fourteen
dollars apiece, and she sells out. She said my problem was never whether people wanted them. My problem was that
I'd been making keepsakes and pricing them like party favors.

Then she looked up how to actually make them properly, because that's what a 34-year-old does while you're
standing there, and she found a little guide, and she sent it to my phone before I'd agreed to anything.

I read the whole thing that night at my kitchen table with the TV off.

The first thing it did was tell me what I'd been doing wrong, which I did not enjoy. Flour and salt was never
going to hold a scent, and it cracks because of what it is, not because of anything I did. A fresh flower pressed
into a wet surface browns because it's still alive. And fragrance added at the wrong moment burns off, which is
exactly why mine smelled wonderful on a Tuesday and like nothing by Saturday.

The second thing it did was tell me what to do instead. Plaster and a silicone mold, so the surface comes out
white and smooth and closed instead of gray and thirsty. Flowers pressed and dried first, so they stay the color
they were. The fragrance oil in at the right temperature, so the scent sits inside the stone and comes out slow
for six to ten weeks. And a plain kraft box with a label, because a beautiful thing in a crinkly cellophane bag
with curly ribbon looks like a church raffle.

Four things. All four of them were fixable in one evening. None of them were talent.

I'm not going to sit here and tell you I'm making a fortune, because I'm not. My first month I sold fourteen
stones and made about a hundred and eighty dollars, most of it to women I work with who had been getting them free
for years and who all, every one of them, said "it's about time." A gift shop in Granville takes thirty a month
now. Most months are a few hundred dollars. I do it two nights a week in front of the television, exactly the way
I always did. The difference is my hands are doing the same thing and the drawer in my kitchen has money in it.

Dale thinks the funniest part is that I didn't change what I make. I changed what I know about what I make.

Judy's in Arizona now. The casita has a little patio and she sits out there in the morning, which she tells me
about at length.

Last month she sent me a picture. It's her new nightstand, and the drawer is open, and the old chipped lavender
one is still in there on its tissue, and next to it is a new one I mailed her in October. Eucalyptus and mint,
white, smooth, with a pressed cornflower that is still blue.

She wrote underneath it: "The new one is nicer. Don't tell the old one."

If you have been making something for years and handing it out because you assumed nobody would pay for it, the
guide Renee sent me is called The Scented Stones Method. It's a short PDF, it's dirt cheap, and I made it back
with my third stone.

Look up what you're giving away before you give away another one.


NATIVE 23 - READS AS HOMEMADE

IMAGE PROMPT (3:4 vertical, phone photo, no faces)
A printed email taped to the inside of a kitchen cupboard door, photographed slightly from below the way you'd
snap it one-handed while standing at the counter. The paper is creased from being folded in four, the tape has
yellowed, and the text is legible, spelled exactly: "Thank you for your interest in the Elkhart Harvest Market.
We are unable to offer you a booth this year. The committee felt the product reads as homemade." Taped just below
it, newer and whiter, a vendor wristband. On the shelf beside the door, a single white scented stone with a
pressed blue flower sits in an open kraft box. Warm kitchen light from the left, visible grain, one corner of the
photo slightly out of focus. No faces, no logos, no other text.

DESCRIPTION

There is a printed email taped inside my kitchen cupboard door. It's been there two years. It's two sentences
long and the second one took something out of me I didn't get back for a while.

"We are unable to offer you a booth this year. The committee felt the product reads as homemade."

My name is Brenda. I'm 58. I live in Elkhart, Indiana, and I do the billing for an auto body shop on Cassopolis
Street, same desk, nineteen years. My husband Ron welded at the RV plant until his shoulder went and now he
mostly fixes other people's mowers in our garage for less than he should charge. Our son Kyle is in Fort Wayne
with two girls under four, so we see them when we see them.

I need to tell you about the Harvest Market before the email makes any sense.

The Elkhart Harvest Market is the first weekend of October, in the lot behind the old library, and it has been
going since before I was born. My mother sold there. Not every year, but the years she had enough put by for the
booth fee. She did braided rag rugs on a frame in our living room, and I sat under the table at that market with a
coloring book when I was six years old, and a woman in a camel coat bought a rug for eleven dollars and my mother
bought us both a caramel apple on the way home and did not stop talking the entire drive.

My mother is 84 now. She's at Hubbard Hill, in the building where they help you with your medications. Her mind
is good. Her hands aren't. She asks me the same question every August, and she asks it the way you ask about
something that's already decided.

"Did you get a table this year, Bren?"

For eleven years I have been making little scented stones at my kitchen table. White, flat, about the size of a
coaster, with a dried flower pressed on top. They go in a drawer or a closet or a car and the space smells like a
garden for weeks. My grandmother made something like them with salt dough and lavender out of her own yard.

And for eleven years I gave every single one of them away. Teachers. Nurses. The girls at the shop. Every casserole
I ever carried into a house after a funeral had one sitting on top of the foil.

Kyle's wife was the first person to say the word sell to me, at Thanksgiving, and I laughed at her. But she kept
at it, and that February I filled out the vendor application for the Harvest Market, and I paid the twenty-dollar
application fee, and I did not tell my mother, because I wanted to walk in there in August and tell her I had a
table at the same market she sold at.

The email came on a Tuesday in March. I read it standing at the counter with my coat still on.

Reads as homemade.

I want to be honest about what that did, because I think a lot of women reading this will know the feeling and
have never had anybody put a name on it. It wasn't that they said no. It was that they said the quiet thing out
loud. Somewhere underneath eleven years of handing those stones to people, I had always suspected mine were the
church-basement version of a real thing. The committee just confirmed it in writing.

I printed it out and taped it inside the cupboard door, which I realize is a strange thing to do. I don't fully
know why I did it. I think I wanted to not be allowed to forget it.

And in August, when my mother asked, I told her the market had gotten very competitive.

Now, I had tried to get better. I want to be clear about that. I'd watched the videos. Flour, salt, warm water, a
few drops of oil. My batches cracked in the pan about a third of the time. The flowers I pressed on went brown
inside a month, which I assumed was just what flowers do. The scent was lovely for about a week and then it was a
white rock in a drawer. I posted a picture in a Facebook craft group asking why mine cracked and got twenty-six
answers, including one woman who told me my water was too hard. I came away from that thinking the gap between
mine and the ones in a boutique in Shipshewana was a thing you either have or you don't.

Here is what changed it, and it was four minutes long.

The first weekend of that October I went to the market anyway, as a customer, because of course I did. And in the
gravel lot afterward I was putting a bag in my trunk two cars down from a woman loading soap crates into a
hatchback. Alicia. She's 34, she makes goat milk soap, and she'd had a booth at the end of the second row.

I don't know what possessed me. I said, "Can I ask you something? I applied and they told me my product reads as
homemade. Do you know what that means?"

She stopped loading.

She said, "Sure I do. It means four things, and all four of them are fixable, and none of them have anything to do
with whether you're good at this."

Then she stood in a parking lot and told me, kindly and fast, like she was reading off a list she'd had to learn
the hard way herself.

My surface. Salt dough dries gray and thirsty and porous, and a porous surface drinks fragrance and chalks up on
your fingers. That's a material problem, not a skill problem. Plaster in a silicone mold comes out white, smooth
and closed.

My flowers. I was pressing live flowers onto a wet surface. Of course they browned. They were still alive. You
press and dry them first, then set them.

My scent. I was adding oil at the wrong moment, so most of it burned off before the thing was even dry. Done right,
the fragrance sits inside the stone and releases for six to ten weeks, and you can bring it back with two drops.

And my packaging, which she was the most blunt about. I had mine in cellophane bags with curly ribbon. She said,
"Brenda, that's a raffle prize. Put it in a kraft box with a paper label and it's a gift."

Then she said the line I've repeated to about forty women since.

"Homemade and handmade are one letter apart and about forty dollars."

She told me there's a guide that walks through all of it, materials, finish, pricing, and how to walk into a shop,
and that she wished she'd had something like that instead of two years of ruining batches. I bought it that night
in the parking lot before I drove home. It cost less than the application fee I'd already lost.

I am not going to tell you the next year was a fairy tale. My first month I sold eleven stones to people I know,
about a hundred and forty dollars, and I undercharged every one of them. I still have a batch out in the garage
that I poured too cold and won't sell to anybody. Most months since have been two or three hundred dollars for two
evenings a week, and a florist on Jackson Boulevard takes a box of twenty when she thinks of it, which is not every
month.

But there are two shops now that reorder without me asking. And I don't wonder anymore whether what I hand
somebody is the real version or the basement version. I know what the difference is. It's four things, and they're
written down.

I applied to the Harvest Market again the following March. I got in. They put me in the second row, four booths
down from Alicia, which I don't think was an accident.

The email is still taped inside my cupboard door. I never took it down. I taped my vendor wristband up underneath
it, which is the only reason I can tell you the exact wording of a two-sentence rejection two years later.

In August my mother asked me the same question she asks every August.

I told her I had a table. The same market, the same lot behind the library. She wanted to know what I'd be selling
them for, so I told her, and she said, "That's more than I ever got for a rug," and she was pleased about it in a
way I'm not going to be able to describe to you.

I brought her a caramel apple on the drive back.

If somebody has told you your work reads as homemade, or you've been quietly telling yourself the same thing, the
guide is called The Scented Stones Method. It's a short PDF, dirt cheap, instant download, and it's the four
things.

It wasn't the idea they turned down. It was the finish.
