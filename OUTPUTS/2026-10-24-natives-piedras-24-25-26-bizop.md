DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There is an empty basket on the counter of the gift shop on Elm Street. It has been empty since August.

There is a card in it, handwritten, in the owner's own handwriting. LOCAL MAKERS - ASK FOR DIANE.

I walked past that basket every week for five months. I assumed it was meant for somebody else. Somebody with a brand. A label. A business name on the side of a van.

Three weeks ago I asked for Diane.

My name is Marcie Dalton. I am fifty-six. I live in Kirksville, Missouri, in a house with a kitchen table that seats six and almost never has six people at it. Nineteen years at the school district office. My husband Dale does heating and air. Our daughter is grown and calls on Sundays.

I am telling you about the basket because almost every town in this country has one, and almost nobody walks up and asks about it.

Here is everything Diane told me, and it took about ninety seconds.

She wants local product on that counter because it is the only thing on her shelves that nobody can order online. She takes it at about half of what she sells it for. She pays inside the week. She reorders when the basket is empty, and she does not want to deal with anybody who disappears for a month.

That is the whole arrangement. No contract. No invoicing software. No business degree.

What I put in that basket is scented stones.

If you have never seen one, it is a small smooth white stone, a little heavier than it looks, with a dried flower pressed into the top. It holds fragrance. You put it in a drawer, a car, a closet, a bathroom, and that space smells like a garden for weeks. No flame. No plug. No wax. Nothing to blow out before bed.

Plaster, fragrance oil, a silicone mould. That is the entire material list. No oven. No kiln. No machine. No skill I did not already have.

I pour them on a Tuesday night with the television on. Fifty at a time. They dry by themselves while I sleep.

Diane's basket takes thirty a month.

And here is the thing that actually rearranged my head. Diane's basket is not the opportunity. Diane's basket is one basket.

Go and count, in your own town, the places that would put a handmade scented stone beside a register. The gift shop. The flower shop. The two hair salons. The nail place. The coffee shop with the little shelf of local honey. The garden centre. The bed and breakfast out on the highway. The gift corner at the hospital.

Then count the people in your town who make them.

Where I live, that number is one. It is me.

Not because I am talented. I got a C in art class twice. It is because almost nobody knows this is something a person can make at a kitchen table, so nobody fills those baskets, so the card sits in there since August.

Let me be specific about what I did not need, because I spent two years losing money on things that needed all of it.

No website. No ads. No inventory bought up front. No shipping. No photographs of my face. No followers. No building an audience. No van. No stall. I have never once posted a product photo.

A woman named Keisha is the reason I know about any of this. She is thirty-four, she rents the booth across from the flower shop, and she sells candles. She told me the stones move faster than her candles do, and she said it like it irritated her.

Now the parts that are not exciting, because you should hear those from me and not find them out on your own.

Shops say no. The hardware store said no. The pharmacy said no in September and yes in October. The first set I carried in to Diane was not good enough and she was kind about it, which was worse than if she had not been.

The gap I had to close was never finding buyers. It was making a stone a shop would actually put next to the register.

My first batch was chalky. The second cracked straight down the middle. I pressed a fresh flower into one and it went brown and ugly in four days. I added the fragrance at the wrong moment and the smell was gone in nine days.

Homemade. That is the word for what I was making. And homemade and handmade are one letter apart and about forty dollars apart.

What closed it was not talent. It was the order of operations. How to get a white, smooth surface with no pinholes. Which flowers survive being pressed and which ones turn brown on you. DESCRIPCION (ingles)

The calendar on my refrigerator is not my family's calendar.

There is nothing on it about birthdays or appointments. It says BELLAMY GIFT CO - RESTOCK 40 on the first. TASHA - BABY SHOWER 60 on the ninth. LEGION HALL FAIR on two Saturdays. And out in the margin, in a box I drew myself, MONROE WEDDING - 120 FAVORS.

Every one of those is somebody who already asked. Not somebody I am hoping will ask.

I am Paula Renfro. Fifty-nine, Chillicothe, Ohio. Twenty-two years at the front desk of a dental office, answering a phone that rang whether I felt like it or not. My husband Ron drives a delivery route. Our son is in Columbus.

I want to show you something about this little business that nobody showed me, and it is the reason I stopped treating it like a hobby.

Demand for handmade things is not luck. It is a calendar. It has a shape, and it is the same shape every year.

October to December, every gift shop, every church bazaar, every craft fair and every office party is buying small handmade presents. That is right now.

January to March, weddings get booked, and a wedding does not order one of anything. It orders a hundred and twenty.

April to June is mothers, teachers, graduations, baby showers. Three gifts a week for people who cannot show up empty handed.

July and August are slower in town, and that is when the farm stands and the tourist places want them.

September, the shops restock for the fall, and it starts again.

There is no month on that calendar where nobody needs a small nice gift. That is the whole reason I picked this.

What I make is scented stones. A small white plaster stone with a dried flower pressed into the top, that holds fragrance and keeps a drawer or a car or a bathroom smelling like a garden for weeks. No flame, no plug, no wax.

Plaster, fragrance oil, a silicone mould. I pour fifty in an evening at my own table. They set overnight on their own while I am asleep. There is no oven and there is nothing to ship.

A woman named Dez told me about them. She is thirty-four and she sells at the Legion hall with her little girl in a wagon beside the table. I asked her what moved best and she did not say candles and she did not say soap.

Now, the part I got wrong for four months, in case it saves you from it.

I was selling them loose. One stone, one person, six dollars, and then standing there waiting for the next person to walk by. I thought my problem was that not enough people came.

My problem was that I was selling a stone.

Nobody is shopping for a stone. They are shopping for a present they do not have to think about. The day I started putting three in a kraft box with a label and a bit of twine, the same woman who used to hand me six dollars handed me twenty-five instead. Same table. Same two minutes of talking.

And a shop cannot sell a loose stone out of a basket. A shop sells sets, because a set already looks like a gift.

That is also how a hundred and twenty favours stops being frightening. You are not making a hundred and twenty decisions. You are making one decision and then pouring for three evenings.

Three questions I get at that table every single Saturday, and I will answer them the way I wish somebody had answered me.

Is it hard? No. It is pouring a thick batter into a tray. If you can make pancakes you can make these. The skill is in four or five details, not in talent.

Do they break? Not if they are cured right. Mine go in a box, in the trunk of a Buick, over the railroad tracks on Western Avenue, and they arrive fine.

Do you need an oven, a kiln, a workshop, a licence, a store? No, no, no, no, and no. I have a kitchen table and a shelf in the laundry room where they sit overnight.

Honest part, and I mean it.

Nobody is getting rich. One Saturday at the Legion hall it rained and I sold nine. A florist told me she already had a vendor. The first baby shower order, I underpriced so badly that I made about four dollars an hour, because I quoted a number off the top of my head with the woman standing in front of me.

That one hurt enough that I went and found out how people who do this properly set a price. There is a short, cheap guide that has the price sheet already worked out, retail, wholesale and event orders, along with the formula, the packaging templates and the scripts for taking a bulk order without losing money on it. It cost me less than a bag of plaster and it is the only reason that MONROE WEDDING box in the margin is written in pen.

To start, you need under fifteen dollars of materials and one evening to see if your hands can do it. That is all I risked.

If you are going to do this, do it now, in October, not in February. The shops are choosing their vendors for the holidays this month and the weddings book right after New Year. A calendar like mine takes about five months to fill up, and it fills up in the order the year does.

The guide is below this post.

And if you want to know whether this is real, do not look at my numbers. Look at your own November. Count how many gifts you personally have to buy before the end of the year, and then multiply that by every house on your street.

I took this picture standing in the air freshener aisle at the Winn-Dixie, holding one of mine up against the shelf.

Plastic plug-ins. Aerosol cans. Cardboard trees for your mirror. A whole wall of it, lit like a dentist's office.

That wall is why I can charge twelve dollars for something that costs me pennies to make, and I am going to explain exactly why, because when I understood it I stopped thinking of this as crafts.

My name is Sharon Kimbrough. Fifty-five, Hattiesburg, Mississippi. I cleaned rooms at a hotel for eleven years and then I did lunches at an elementary school. My husband Curtis is on disability since his back went. I have two grown boys and a dog named Peaches who is afraid of the broom.

It holds fragrance and it keeps a drawer, a car, a closet or a bathroom smelling like a garden for weeks. Plaster, fragrance oil, a silicone mould, poured at my kitchen table. Fifty in an evening. They dry overnight while I sleep. No oven, no machine, no experience.

Here is what the wall at the Winn-Dixie tells you.

Everybody wants their house to smell nice. That is not a trend and it is not a niche. It is every house.

And look at what they are being offered. Something plastic they have to plug into a wall. Something in an aerosol can that lasts ten minutes. A candle you cannot leave burning and cannot put in an apartment that bans them, or a nursery, or a car.

Nobody walks that aisle and thinks, this is lovely, this is what I want in my bedroom.

So when somebody is handed a small stone that looks like something out of a nice hotel, that they can set in a drawer and forget, with no flame and no plug and no outlet, they do not ask what it costs to make. They ask if you have the lavender one.

A gift shop sells it for twelve and takes it from me at around six. A woman at a fair buys three in a box for twenty-five. A wedding needs a hundred and twenty of them and the mother of the bride is relieved to find anybody at all.

That is the gap. Enormous everyday want, and the only things filling it are plastic and fire.

A girl named Camille, who is thirty-one and does nails two chairs down from my sister, is the one who put a stone in my hand in the first place. She had one in her station drawer. I thought it was soap.

My first stones smelled wonderful for about a week and then they smelled like nothing.

I thought I had been sold a fairy tale. I had twenty-two of them in a shoebox that were, essentially, rocks.

The fragrance was not the problem. The timing was. There is a specific moment in the pour when the oil goes in, and a ratio, and a way of curing them so the scent sits inside the stone instead of flashing off the surface. Get it wrong and you have a week. Get it right and you have six to ten weeks, and the customer can revive it with two drops and come back to you anyway when they want a new one.

Nobody could have guessed that. I certainly did not. It was written down in a short, dirt cheap guide by a woman who had already ruined more batches than I had, and it also had the supplier list, the moulds, the finishes and the price sheet.

And a group of women from my sister's prayer circle who order for every single baby that gets born in this town.

I have never shipped a box. I have never run an advertisement. I have never put my face on anything. There is no website with my name on it, and there is no shop with my name on the window.

Honest, now. Nobody is getting rich in a month. I sell out at some fairs and at one in August I sold four. Curtis thinks the whole house smells like a funeral parlour on pour nights. And it is work, it is just easy work that you can do sitting down.

What it takes to find out if you can do it: under fifteen dollars of plaster, oil and a mould, and one evening.

And it is October. Every gift in the country gets bought in the next nine weeks.
