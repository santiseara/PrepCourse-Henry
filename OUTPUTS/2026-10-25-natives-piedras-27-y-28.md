DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There is a scented stone on a shelf at a home store forty minutes from my house with a twenty-eight dollar tag hanging off it.

I make the same thing on my kitchen table for pocket change.

I am not telling you that because I think I am clever. I am telling you because of what that tag actually means, and I had to have it explained to me by a twenty-nine year old before I understood it.

My name is Rhonda Mabry. I am fifty-seven. Cullman, Alabama. Fourteen years behind the register at a pharmacy, which means I have watched what people buy when they have ten dollars left and when they have a hundred. My husband Verlon drives a forklift at the co-op. Our boy is in Huntsville now and calls on Sundays.

My niece Kayla works at that store. She is the one who sent me the picture in the first place, with a message that said "isn't this what you make".

So I asked her the question I would want somebody to ask. Do they actually sell?

She said they restock that tray twice a week. She said people pick one up, smell it, and ask if there are other scents. She said the dried flower ones go first.

Here is what that means, and it is the whole reason I am writing this.

You are not being asked to invent a market. You are not being asked to convince anybody of anything. A chain with a buying department and a spreadsheet already decided this product sells, and they gave it shelf space to prove it.

The only thing missing is somebody local who makes them.

Because here is the part that chain cannot do. It cannot be from here. The gift shop on the square, the flower shop, the two salons, the coffee place with the little shelf of local honey, the bed and breakfast out on the highway. Every one of those wants something on the counter that nobody can order online, and that is the only thing they have that the chain store does not.

In my town, the number of people making these is one. It is me. Not because I am talented. I failed home economics.

If you have never seen one, it is a small smooth white stone, heavier than it looks, with a dried flower pressed into the top. It holds fragrance. You put it in a drawer, a car, a closet, a bathroom, and that space smells like a garden for weeks. No flame. No wax. No plug. Nothing to blow out before bed.

Plaster, fragrance oil, a silicone mould. That is the whole material list. No oven. No kiln. No machine. No experience I did not already have.

I pour them on a Tuesday night with the television on. They set by themselves while I sleep. The next morning I turn the tray over and they fall out.

Now the part I would want to know before I spent a dollar, and nobody told me.

My first ones did not look like the one on that shelf. They looked homemade. There were little pinholes all over the surface. The second batch cracked right down the middle. I pressed a fresh flower into one and it went brown and ugly in four days. And one tray smelled beautiful for a week and then smelled like nothing at all.

Homemade and handmade are one letter apart and about forty dollars apart.

What closed that gap was not skill. It was the order of operations. How to get a smooth white surface with no pinholes. Which flowers survive being pressed and which turn brown on you. When exactly the fragrance goes in so it lasts weeks instead of days. How to box three of them so they look like something a person already paid for.

All of that is written down. There is a short guide by a woman who ruined far more batches than I did, and it costs less than the bag of plaster. Formula, supplier list, finishes, packaging, a price sheet for retail and wholesale and event orders, and the exact words to say when you walk into a shop with three samples in a box. That last part is what I would have got wrong on my own, because I am not a saleswoman and I never will be.

Honest part, because you should hear it from me.

Shops say no. The hardware store said no. The pharmacy where I worked for fourteen years said no in September and yes in October, and that one stung. Nobody is handing you thousands of dollars in your first month, and anyone who tells you different is selling you something worse than a bag of plaster.

What it is, is repeatable. The fragrance fades after about two months, which means their customers come back in, which means the shop texts me instead of me chasing anybody.

To find out whether you can do this at all, you need one evening and less than fifteen dollars of materials. That is the entire test. If your hands cannot do it, you lost the price of two coffees.

And this is the month that matters. Right now every shop in your town is deciding what sits beside the register for the holidays. In January the wedding orders get booked, and one wedding is eighty to a hundred and fifty favours. By February those counters are spoken for, for the year.

The link to the guide is below this post.

Last Saturday I drove back out to that store and put one of mine on the shelf next to theirs, just to look at the two of them together. Mine was the one with the lavender.

Then I took it back, because I had somewhere to be with it on Monday.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

This is my cash box at four in the afternoon, at the end of a craft fair in a parking lot.

The money is in there. But the money is not the part that mattered.

The part that mattered is sitting on top of it. A business card from a gift shop on the other side of the county. The back of a receipt with WEDDING - JUNE - 140 written on it. And a piece of notebook paper with a phone number from a woman who does the flowers for every funeral and every wedding at her church.

I went to that fair thinking it was a day of selling. It is not. I had it backwards for most of a year.

My name is Glenda Prewitt. Fifty-eight. Vandalia, Illinois. Sixteen years on the packing line at a food plant, second shift, and my knees will tell you about all sixteen. My husband Wendell works on farm machinery. We have a granddaughter who thinks my work table is hers.

What I sell is scented stones. A small white plaster stone with a dried flower pressed into the top, that holds fragrance and keeps a drawer or a car or a closet smelling like a garden for weeks. No flame, no wax, no plug. Plaster, fragrance oil, a silicone mould, poured at my own table with the television on. They set overnight by themselves. No oven and nothing to ship.

Now let me tell you what a fair actually is, because this is the part nobody said out loud to me.

A fair is not where you sell forty stones to forty strangers. A fair is where the people who buy in bulk come to find somebody who makes things.

Who walks through a craft fair with a purpose? The woman who owns a gift shop and needs something on her counter that nobody can order online. The mother of a bride, nine months out, who has a list and a budget and is dreading it. The woman who organises the church bazaar. The one who does corporate gifts for an insurance office at Christmas. The bed and breakfast owner. The event planner who got sent by somebody else.

They are not there for a nice afternoon. They are there shopping for a supplier.

Forty strangers buying one stone each is a nice Saturday. One gift shop that reorders every month is next year, and the June wedding on the back of that receipt is a hundred and forty pieces from one phone call.

There is something else I noticed at my second fair, and you can check this at any fair in your area this weekend without spending a dollar.

Look at the vendor list. Count the jewellery tables. Count the candles, the soap, the crocheted blankets, the wood signs, the honey. Then count how many people are selling what I sell.

Every fair I have done, that number has been zero or one. Nine jewellery tables and nobody with a stone. That is not a coincidence, it is just that almost nobody knows this can be made at a kitchen table.

A woman named Priya had the table next to mine at the fairgrounds in July. She is thirty, she sells macrame, and she is the one who rearranged my head. I asked her how she does on a day like that and she said the fair does not pay her. The two shops she picked up at a fair in March pay her. She goes to fairs to meet buyers, and she said it like it was obvious, because for her it was.

So here is what I had been getting wrong, and it was not the product.

I was setting out a basket of loose stones with a little sign. A shop owner cannot do anything with a basket of loose stones. She needs to see a set, she needs to know what it costs her by the dozen, and she needs to believe you will still be answering your phone in March.

The day I put three in a kraft box with a label and twine, and had a price list folded in the cash box for anybody who asked about quantity, the same table with the same stones started producing those pieces of paper instead of only coins.

The other thing I got wrong was the stones themselves, early on. Mine had pinholes. One flower turned brown in four days. A whole tray smelled wonderful for a week and then smelled like nothing, which is the one that nearly made me quit, because I thought I had been lied to about the whole idea.

None of that was talent. It was the order of operations, a ratio, and the moment the fragrance goes in. It is all written down in a short guide by a woman who had already ruined more batches than I had, along with the supplier list, the moulds and finishes, the packaging, the wholesale and event price sheet, and what to say when a shop owner stops at your table and asks what you would charge her for thirty.

It cost me less than the bag of plaster, and it is the reason there is pen on the back of that receipt instead of a maybe.

Honest, now. One fair in August it rained and I sold nine stones and came home with a wet tablecloth and nothing on top of the money. The first big order I quoted, I quoted off the top of my head with the woman standing in front of me, and I made about four dollars an hour on it. A florist told me she already had a vendor and that was that.

And a booth is not free. It is the only real cost in this whole thing, and some Saturdays it does not come back.

What it takes to find out if this is for you: one evening and under fifteen dollars of plaster, oil and a mould. Then one table at the next fair in your area.

If you are going to do it, now is the moment, not February. The holiday fairs are being booked this month and the weddings get booked right after New Year, and those orders go to whoever was standing there with a box of samples.

The link to the guide is below this post.

I still have that cash box. The money from that Saturday is long gone, same as the money from any Saturday.

The card, the receipt and the notebook paper are in a tin on the shelf above my work table, and two of the three are still buying from me.
