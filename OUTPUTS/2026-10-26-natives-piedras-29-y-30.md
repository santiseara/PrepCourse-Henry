DESCRIPCION (ingles)

This is my phone at ten past seven on a Monday morning. I had not touched it yet. Nineteen of those are people asking me for more.

I sold out of a catalog for nine years. Cosmetics first, then candles. Nine years of asking people to buy something, of leaving a book on a break room table and hoping, of texting a woman twice and feeling bad about the second text. In nine years I never once woke up to this.

My name is Wanda Prewitt. I am fifty-four. Sapulpa, Oklahoma. My husband Dale drives a water truck and is gone four days at a stretch. Our girl Krista is twenty-six and lives in Tulsa, our boy is in the Guard, and the dog is named Chigger because he was small and would not let go.

What is on the table is plaster. That is all. Plaster, a little fragrance oil, a silicone mold. You stir it in a bowl, you pour it, you go to bed, and in the morning it is a solid white stone that smells like a garden and keeps smelling for weeks. No oven. No wax. No fire. Nothing plugged into a wall.

I did not start this to make money. I made a batch because my sister was having an open house and I wanted to bring something that was not a candle, since everyone brings a candle.

Eleven women asked me where I got them. Two asked if I would make some for them. One of them handed me cash in my sister's kitchen before I had agreed to anything.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part that people get wrong about me. I did not sell anything that night. I stood there with a plate of them and answered questions. That is the whole difference between this and the nine years before it.

Selling out of a catalog is standing at a door knocking. This was answering a door that was already being knocked on.

Now here is the part nobody tells you, and the part that cost me two months.

My first batches were not sellable. They were homemade. There is a difference between homemade and handmade and it is not a small one. Mine had little pinholes all over the sides, like the surface of a cracker. The bottoms were slightly gray instead of white. And worst of all, they smelled wonderful for about two days and then they smelled like nothing at all.

I thought I had bought cheap fragrance. I bought more expensive fragrance. Same thing. Strong for two days, gone by Thursday.

It was not the fragrance. It was when I was putting it in.

I found that out from Alexis, who is twenty-eight and sells at a market two towns over and is friends with my daughter. I asked her, half joking, what I was doing wrong. She did not explain it to me. She told me there was a short guide she had used, written by a woman who had been making and selling these for years, and that it covered exactly that, the timing, and the reason the surface pits, and the finish on the bottom.

I will be honest with you, I almost did not buy it. I have bought a lot of things that were going to change my life. A kit once, for two hundred dollars, that is still in a closet with the shrink wrap on part of it.

This was not that. It was short and it was specific and it was written like a woman telling you how she does it at her own table. The timing of the oil. The water ratio, which is the whole reason the pits happen. How to get a flat white bottom. Which dried flowers hold their color and which ones turn brown on you in three weeks and make the whole thing look old. How to package three in a box so it stops looking like a craft and starts looking like a product.

The first batch I poured after reading it came out smooth. All of them. I sat at this table and turned them over one at a time looking for the pits and there were none.

And then the thing I did not expect at all.

The scent held. Week one, week two, week five. A woman who bought three in August messaged me in October to say the one in her car was still going and could she get three more for her sister.

That is what is on my phone up there. It is not nineteen new people who found me. A lot of those are the same people, again. That is what I did not understand about this product for the first few months. It runs out. It is not a thing you sell somebody once and then go looking for a stranger. It fades, and they come back, and they bring somebody.

I make them at this table at night when Dale is out on a run. A dozen or so at a time, which is about as long as a show. They set while I sleep. In the morning they come out of the mold and I put them on a towel right about where that paper towel is in the picture.

Let me tell you what this is and what it is not.

It is not a business that is going to replace what Dale brings home. I am not going to sit here and tell you I got rich pouring plaster in my kitchen. The first money I made from it, I put forty dollars of gas in Krista's car and I bought the good coffee instead of the can.

What it is, is the first thing I have ever done where I am not the one asking. For nine years I was the one asking. Now I am the one with nineteen messages and a notebook, deciding which Saturday I can do.

I will tell you the two questions I get, since you probably have them.

No, you do not have to be crafty. I am not. I cannot draw, I cannot sew, my mother used to say I had hands for carrying and that is about it. This is measuring and pouring. If you can follow a recipe for cornbread you can do this.

And no, you do not need a shop or a website or a single advertisement. I have never run one. Everything on that screen came from somebody who smelled one at somebody else's house, or picked one up off a counter.

If you want to know what I use, it is that guide. It is a short read, it is dirt cheap, and it is the whole reason my stones stopped looking like something a child made at camp. The link is below this post.

Here is the last thing, and then I will let you go.

That phone is still sitting against the sugar bowl. I am going to answer those nineteen tonight, after I pour. There is a page in that notebook with three Saturdays written on it and one of them is already full.

Nine years I kept a catalog in my purse in case somebody asked. Nobody ever asked.

Nineteen asked while I was asleep.

- Wanda P., Sapulpa, Oklahoma

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

Those are on the top shelf of my closet, where the kids do not look because the kids never look up.

Four bags. Tissue still folded. Receipt still in the one on the end. Bought in one trip, on a Tuesday, in September, with money that did not come out of our account and that I did not have to ask anybody for.

If that does not sound like much to you, then you have never stood in a store in December doing math.

My name is Charlene Boggs. I am fifty-five. Hiawassee, Georgia, which is a town where the same forty cars go by. I have run a register at the same grocery store for a long time. Ron is at the tire shop. Tyler is sixteen and eats like a truck driver, Hannah is thirteen and has opinions, and our oldest is out of the house now and calls on Sundays.

I am a cashier, so I will tell you something about Christmas that cashiers know and nobody says out loud. We can tell. Not in a judging way, not ever in a judging way. But when somebody is putting the gift cards back one at a time, or asking me to ring up the groceries first and then stop, we can tell. I have been the woman asking to ring the groceries first.

So this is not a post about getting rich. It is a post about a shelf in a closet.

What is on the edge of that shelf, the little white one with the flower on it, is what paid for those bags.

It is a scented stone. Plaster, a few drops of fragrance oil, a silicone mold, poured at night at my kitchen table and solid by morning. No oven. No candle, no wax, no fire, nothing you plug in. It sits on a shelf or in a car or in a drawer and the whole room smells like it for weeks.

I did not go looking for this. My sister talked me into putting a few on her table at a church craft sale because she had a gap to fill. I made eighteen of them and I was embarrassed about half of them.

They went in under an hour. The woman who bought the last four asked me if I had more at home, and I said no, and she asked for my number instead.

I am going to be honest about the next part, because I think it is the part that matters.

That first batch was not good. They went because the table was busy and because they smelled nice right then. Up close, mine had little holes in the surface. One cracked in my hand when I was packing it. And the dried flowers I pressed on top turned brown and sad within a month, which I did not find out until a woman politely told me so at the register, holding one in her palm.

That is the gap nobody warns you about. The difference between something you made and something somebody is happy to pay for is small, but it is real, and you cannot guess your way to it. I tried for most of a summer.

What closed it for me was not me. It was Bree, my nephew's wife, who is thirty and sells at a farmers market up in Blairsville. I showed her my pitiful tray, and instead of telling me it was nice, which is what everybody else did, she told me exactly what was wrong with it and then told me where she had learned it. A short guide, written by a woman who makes these and sells them, dirt cheap, nothing fancy.

I read the whole thing in one sitting at this kitchen table.

Three things in it changed everything for me. How much water, and why that is the only reason the surface pits. When the fragrance goes in, which is the reason mine smelled strong for two days and then quit. And which flowers keep their color, and how to press them so they stay.

The batch after that came out smooth and white and every one of them was sellable. I sat here turning them over and I got a little emotional about it, which I am not going to pretend I did not.

Then I did the only clever thing I have done in this whole story. Instead of selling them one at a time at the register like a woman with a tote bag, I started putting three in a little kraft box with a tag. The same stones. People paid more for the box than they ever paid me for three loose ones, and they bought them as gifts, which means they bought them in multiples.

By July I had a rhythm. Pour on a weeknight while Hannah does homework at the other end of the table. Pack on Sunday. Hand them off at the store, at church, at the ball field, to women who ask me and to women their friends send.

I am not going to tell you a number here, because it was not a big number and because I do not think numbers are the point. Here is the point. In September, for the first time since Tyler was in diapers, I did my Christmas shopping in September. On a Tuesday. In one trip. In an actual store, not from a clearance rack in January and hidden for eleven months.

It is like layaway in reverse. Instead of paying a little every week toward something I owed, I made a little every week and the gifts were already paid for before anyone asked for them.

Let me answer what you are thinking, because I thought it too.

No, I am not crafty. I run a register. I cannot sew, I did not do art, and my handwriting is bad. This is measure, stir, pour, wait. That is the entire skill.

No, I do not have a website or a shop or any kind of ad. I have a phone and a town of forty cars.

And no, you do not need a pile of money to find out if you can do it. The materials for my first real batch cost less than what I spend on a week of lunches at work.

What I would do, if I were you and you are sitting there in October the way I was sitting there in June, is get that guide and pour one batch this week. Not a business. One batch. See them come out of the mold in the morning and see what the first person says when they smell one.

The link is below this post.

Those four bags are still up there. Nobody in this house knows they exist. The tissue is still folded, the receipt is still in the one on the end, and the stone I left on the edge of that shelf is doing its job, because every time I open that closet the whole thing smells like lavender.

On the morning of the twenty-fifth that shelf is going to be empty, and I am the one who filled it.

- Charlene B., Hiawassee, Georgia
