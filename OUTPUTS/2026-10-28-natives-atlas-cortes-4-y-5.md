NATIVE 4 - THE THURSDAYS THAT STOPPED
(angulo: la que no vuelve / el corte a las tres semanas - territorio del AT-V16)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

The left page is dense with handwritten names in four or five different pens, written in six-week columns going back months, two of them crossed out. The right page is almost empty: ruled lines with nothing on them. A brown coffee ring across the corner, reading glasses folded on the page, a chewed pencil in the spine, cutting shears and a carbon comb lying beside the book, a few cut hairs on the counter. Warm late-afternoon light from a storefront window on the right, the shadow of the phone falling across the empty page. No people, no faces, no brand names, no printed text. Handwriting only, not legible as specific words. Shot on an older phone, slightly soft and grainy, a real small-town salon counter with scratches and marker stains, not a studio, not styled.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

Fourteen women stopped rebooking with me in one year and not a single one of them ever complained about her hair.

I only found out because I keep a paper book. I am going to tell you what I saw when I sat down and went through it, because it took me thirty-four years to understand what I was looking at.

My name is Dee Ann Kowalczyk. I am fifty-seven. I have been licensed since 1992 and I have rented the first station, the one by the window, in the same four-chair shop in Sheboygan, Wisconsin, for twenty-one years.

My work is good. I need to say that before the rest of this, because what comes next sounds like a woman running herself down, and it is not that.

Here is what a paper book looks like after twenty-one years.

A name, then six weeks, then the same name. Then six weeks, then the same name. For some of these women that pattern goes back further than my son's marriage.

And then, on some of them, the pattern just stops.

No argument. No phone call. No bad review, because women around here do not leave bad reviews, they leave.

For a long time I had reasons for it. People move. The new place out by the highway does blowouts. Somebody's daughter got licensed and now she does the whole family in her kitchen. All of that is true and all of it happens.

But in one year it was fourteen, and eleven of the fourteen had one thing in common that I did not want to see.

Every single one of them had asked me for something a little different the last time she sat down.

Let me tell you about Marilyn.

Marilyn Dauch. Sixty-three. Retired school secretary, three grandsons, every sixth Thursday at two o'clock since 2009. She brings me rhubarb out of her garden in June whether I ask for it or not, and she tells me the same story about the rhubarb every June.

Two autumns ago Marilyn sat down and said she wanted a long bob with a longer piece at the front. She had seen it on a woman at church.

I did it. I did not steer her off it, I did not soften it, I gave her exactly what she asked for and it was beautiful. She turned her head both ways in the mirror. She touched the front piece. She tipped me more than she ever has.

Three weeks later I saw her at the credit union.

The cut was gone. Not grown out. Gone. The front had slid down and lost the angle. The back had sat down flat against her neck and the whole shape had collapsed into the same bob she had been wearing since the Obama administration.

She was perfectly nice to me. She asked about my son. She did not mention her hair, and I did not mention her hair, and she never booked again.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part I got wrong for three decades.

That was not a bad haircut. On the day, that was one of the best cuts I have ever done.

A haircut has two days. It has the day you do it, which is the day you get judged on, and it has day twenty-one, which is the day she has to live in it with no stylist and no round brush and eleven minutes before work.

I was cutting the day one shape. I had spent thirty-four years getting very good at the day one shape.

And the thing that decides day twenty-one is not the finish. It is not the texturizing, it is not the product, it is not how good it looked when I spun the chair around.

It is where I sectioned, what degree I held it at, and whether my guide stayed put or traveled.

Those three decisions are made in the first ten minutes, before anything looks like anything, and they are the only three that are still there three weeks later. Nobody ever looks at the back of a haircut on day twenty-one except the woman who has to wear it.

So for years I fixed the wrong end.

I bought better shears. Twice. The second pair cost more than my first car payment and they are wonderful and they did not change one thing about week three.

I started cutting everything dry so I could see it. That helped the day one shape get even better, which, as it turns out, was the problem wearing a different coat.

I put more layers in things. If a shape collapsed I added movement, which is a word I used for years without being able to tell you where the layers were supposed to start.

I asked women to come in every four weeks instead of six, which is a thing you can only ask so many times before you hear it in your own voice.

And I took pictures. This is the one that embarrasses me. For about six years I took a phone picture of the back of every head before the woman stood up. I have hundreds of them. I have a photo album on my phone with hundreds of pictures of day one.

Not one picture of day twenty-one. I had a perfect record of the only day that was already going fine.

Here is where it turned, and it was not in a classroom.

Last March I got sent to a continuing education day in a hotel meeting room in Green Bay. Folding chairs, bad coffee, a man with a microphone selling color.

At my table there was a woman named Robin who does hair out of a van. Nursing homes, home visits, two days a week in people's kitchens. She is maybe thirty-five and she does more heads in a week than I do.

She has no station, no mirror she trusts, no second chance. She cannot say "come back Tuesday and I will even it up." She cuts it once, in somebody's living room, under a ceiling fan.

Halfway through the morning she put her phone on the table with a page open on it. A head drawn from the back, sections drawn right on it, a number for the angle, an arrow showing where the guide came from, and the steps in order down the side.

I asked her what that was for and she said something I have thought about every working day since.

She said, "I am not fixing it at the end anymore. I decide it at the top, and then I leave it alone."

That is the entire difference between her week three and my week three, and she said it like she was telling me the time.

What she had is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. One page per cut. Twenty-five of them.

It is not a course. There are no modules, nothing to finish, nothing to log into, nothing to sign up for and nobody above you. It is a reference. You open the page for the cut you are about to do and you close it.

Now the honest part, because I was not a believer on day one.

The first page I used was for a long bob with a diagonal forward line, which is Marilyn's cut, which I have done maybe four hundred times.

The page had the back at zero degrees with a stationary guide at the nape. I have been traveling that guide upward for eleven years because it leaves it softer and women like soft.

I stood in my own shop and argued with a piece of paper in my head for about five seconds. Then I did it the page's way, because I had already decided I was going to do what it said for one month.

Day one, it looked less soft than mine. I want to be honest about that. I did not love it.

That woman came back in six weeks and the line was still there. Still. There. Three weeks into it she had the same haircut I gave her, which in thirty-four years is not a sentence I have been able to say very often.

The second one was a hush cut, which I had never done, with the sectioning map and the order to follow printed on one page beside me. Fifty minutes. No pauses, no standing there deciding, no chasing.

That is my whole story. One cut I had been doing wrong for eleven years and one cut I had never done.

If you are where I was, here is the short version of what I needed and never had:

It has to show me the back of the head, not the front, because the front is the part I can already see.
It has to tell me where the guide comes from and whether it moves, in words, not in vibes.
It has to give me the degree written down, because "lift slightly" is not a number.
It has to be readable cold, in under a minute, with wet hands, in my own shop with nobody standing behind me.
It has to tell me what the cut does in three weeks, not how to style it on day one.

The Atlas does all five, which is why I am still using it in the middle of a Tuesday instead of writing about it.

It is dirt cheap, which annoys me a little when I think about those shears.

Marilyn is in my book again. Her daughter booked her in April and she did not say why and I did not ask why. She sat down and asked for the long bob with the longer front and I said yes, and before I picked up my shears I looked at a page on my phone for about thirty seconds.

She was looking at her own reflection. She has never once asked me what I was looking at.

There are a lot of blank lines on the right side of my book that are not blank anymore.

Dee Ann K.
Licensed cosmetologist, 34 years
Sheboygan, Wisconsin

P.S. If you are reading this and thinking your retention is fine because nobody complains, I need you to hear me: nobody complains. That is the whole thing. A woman who is unhappy with her hair does not tell her hairdresser, because she likes her hairdresser. She just books somewhere else and then she is a little awkward with you at the grocery store for a year.
P.P.S. On being a finisher. I was one. I cut dry, I corrected as I went, I prided myself on being able to save anything at the end. Correcting at the end is how you get a shape that only exists on the day you made it. Everything that survives week three was decided before the first section was even wet, and that is the part I never had written down anywhere.
P.P.P.S. I assumed this was for girls just out of school. It is the opposite. A new stylist does not know enough to have habits. I had eleven years of a traveling guide on a cut that needed a stationary one, and I was never going to find that on my own, because to me it was just how the cut is done.
P.P.P.P.S. It is not a video library. I have paid for two of those. A video is somebody else's hands moving at their speed while mine are busy. This is a page, and a page waits for you.

NATIVE 5 - THE MANNEQUIN AT THE BACK OF MY SHELF
(angulo: la carta que se achica / "yo no hago pelo corto" - la identidad construida sobre un no)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist standing in the back room of a small salon, looking at a wooden shelf at eye level. On the shelf, three mannequin practice heads with long hair, all turned away so only the backs of their heads and their napes are visible, and behind them, pushed into the corner, a fourth head with a short cropped pixie, dusty, half hidden by the others. Around them: a cardboard box of perm rods, a stack of folded towels, a clamp stand, an old spray bottle gone cloudy. Dim light from a small window with bent blinds, dust visible in the air, the phone shadow across the shelf. No people, no faces, no visible mannequin faces, no brand names, no text. Shot on an older phone, slightly soft, real back room with chipped paint and a water stain, not a studio, not styled.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There is a mannequin head at the back of my shelf with a pixie on it. I cut that pixie in 2007 and I did not cut another short haircut on a paying woman for nineteen years after it.

I want to tell you about the sentence I used for all nineteen of those years, because I know for a fact that half of you have a version of it.

My name is Darlene Vick. I am sixty-one. I have been licensed since 1988 and I have worked the window chair in a three-chair shop next to a laundromat in Cape Girardeau, Missouri, for twenty-three years.

I am not a bad hairdresser. I am a very good long-hair stylist, and I want you to notice how nice that sounds, because I built that sentence on purpose and then I lived inside it.

The sentence I used was, "Oh honey, I'm not really a short hair person."

Say it out loud. It sounds like taste. It sounds like a style, like preferring one color line over another. It sounds like something you chose.

It is a door, and every time you say it you close it a little further, and after enough years the door is the wall.

It never happens all at once. In 2009 I sent a woman down the street for a pixie. Then a girl wanted what we were all calling a bixie and I did not take it. Then short bobs with any real graduation in the back. Then that shaggy short thing everybody wanted two summers ago, with the short pieces at the front.

Each one on its own is nothing. You are busy, she is young, there is a girl six blocks away who is better at it.

Add them up over nineteen years and the cuts I do not do are not a gap in my book anymore. They are who I am. There are women in this town who have never once thought of me for anything above the shoulder.

Let me tell you about Tonya.

Tonya Breheny. Thirty-eight. She works nights at the hospital and she has had the same long layers from me since 2016, and she is the kind of woman who says thank you three times on her way out the door.

She turned forty in February. No, I am wrong, she turned forty last February, and she came in two weeks before it with her phone already out.

She wanted it all off. A bixie, short in the back, longer pieces through the top. She had three pictures saved and she had clearly been looking at them for a long time, because she knew the words for it.

And I heard my own voice say, "Oh honey, I'm not really a short hair person."

She said that was okay. That is what they always say. She let me trim the ends she had come in to lose, she tipped me, she said thank you three times.

Three weeks later I saw her at the Dairy Queen on Kingshighway with the bixie. Somebody else did it, and whoever it was did it well.

She was so happy she hugged me. She hugged me, in the parking lot, wearing the haircut I would not give her.

I drove home and I did not turn the radio on.

Here is what I finally understood that night, and it is not what I had been telling myself.

It was never that I could not cut short hair. My hands are thirty-eight years old at this and they are good.

It is that short hair has no margin.

On long hair I can be a little wrong for forty minutes and still land it. The length forgives me. I can correct, blend, soften, re-check and nobody alive can tell where I changed my mind.

On a short cut, the whole thing is decided in the first three sections. Where you start, what you hold the perimeter at, where your guide sits and whether it moves. Get those wrong and you are not correcting anything, you are negotiating with what is left.

And I did not have those first three sections written down anywhere, for any short shape, ever. Not in my head, not on paper, nowhere.

So I had built nineteen years of personality on top of a gap in the first ten minutes of a haircut.

I did try. I want that on the record.

In 2014 I drove to Saint Louis for a two-day cutting class. I cut a beautiful pixie there. Beautiful. There was a man standing behind my shoulder saying "good, now over-direct that up and back," and I did, and it was lovely.

That man does not live in Cape Girardeau. At home, alone, at ten in the morning, with a woman watching my face in the mirror, that man is not there, and I never once reproduced that haircut.

I watched videos. A video is somebody else's hands going at their speed while mine are holding a comb. By the time I scrub back to the part I needed, I have already cut it.

I bought a big photo book of hairstyles, and this is the one that really gets me now. It was all finished cuts, photographed from the front, with the lighting done. It is the result. Everybody can see the result. Nobody ever shows you the back of the head with the sections drawn on it, which is the actual job.

I practiced on mannequins. Mannequin hair does not shrink, does not have a cowlick, does not have a bald spot she is sensitive about, and does not look at you.

What finally moved me was not a stylist at all.

There is a barber next to the laundromat, a man in his forties, fades all day long. Last spring I watched him pull a plastic card out of his apron pocket, look at it for a few seconds, put it back and start cutting.

I asked him, honestly a little smug about it, whether he still needed that.

He said, "I don't need it. I use it. That is not the same thing."

He told me he wrote his own because the first year he was guessing and his line got away from him on a kid's head and the mother is still cold to him in the Walmart.

A man with a laminated card in his apron, and I had been standing there for nineteen years with nothing, calling it taste.

What I use now is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. One page per cut, twenty-five of them, and there is a whole block of them for short shapes. Pixie with a fringe, the bixie, a graduated bob, the short shag.

Each page is one page. The shape from the back. The sectioning drawn on the head. The elevation written as a number. Where the guide starts and whether it travels. The steps in order, one through eight. And the mistake people make, printed next to the step where they make it, which is the single most useful thing anybody has ever handed me.

It is not a course. Nothing to finish, nothing to log into, nothing to sign up for and nobody above you.

The honest part.

I took that 2007 mannequin head down off the shelf and dusted her off and did the pixie page on her, cold, by myself, on a Monday when we were closed.

The page starts at the nape with a stationary guide and over-directs everything up to it. I have always started at the top and worked down, because that is how I was taught in 1988 and I never had a reason to ask.

I stood in an empty shop and argued with a page for a few seconds like a crazy woman. Then I did it its way.

The nape sat flat. The crown had actual weight in it instead of that hollow thing that happens to me on short hair. I did it again on Tuesday before we opened to see if it was luck.

The first live one was Ruthie, who is seventy-four and had been asking me for "something easier" for two years while I gave her the same set every time.

Short graduated shape, fifty minutes, two looks at the page. She touched the back of her neck in the mirror and said, "Well, Darlene." That is all she said. Fifty years of that woman's approval in two words.

If I had known what to look for, this is what I would have been looking for:

One page, not a chapter. If I have to scroll, I have already picked up my shears.
The back and the perimeter, drawn, because that is where short cuts are won and lost.
Where to start, and what the guide does after that. Not "follow your guide." Where it is and whether it moves.
Usable cold, the first time, alone, with no one standing behind me saying "good, now over-direct."
The mistake told to me before I make it, not explained to me after.

Five things. It does all five, and it is dirt cheap, which I think about every time I remember the gas money to Saint Louis.

The mannequin is not at the back of the shelf anymore. She is on the counter by the window and the pixie on her is from this year.

Tonya came back in September. She let me take it shorter through the back and I checked the page in front of her while she was still in the shampoo chair, and she did not notice, because what a woman sees when you take a few seconds before you start is a hairdresser being careful with her.

I do short hair now. Sixty-one years old, thirty-eight years licensed, and that is a new sentence for me.

Darlene V.
Licensed cosmetologist, 38 years
Cape Girardeau, Missouri

P.S. If you have a sentence like mine, and most of us do, I am not going to stand here and tell you it is a lie. "I don't really do curly." "I'm not a blonde girl." "I don't do men." I am just telling you what mine cost, and it was not money. It was Tonya hugging me in a parking lot.
P.P.S. On whether using a reference makes you less of an artist. The barber next door said it better than I can. He does not need it. He uses it. The haircut does not know whether you remembered it or read it, and the woman in the chair does not know either. She only knows whether she got what she asked for.
P.P.P.S. On being too old for this. I had the exact same thought and I had it in the voice of my first boss in 1988. I am sixty-one. It took me one closed Monday and a dusty mannequin head to change a thing I had believed about myself since the second Bush was president. There is no version of the rest of my career where I would rather have kept the sentence.
P.P.P.P.S. On what it is not. It is not a certification and there is no test. It is not a video library, it is not Pinterest photos, and nobody is going to email you about your progress. It is twenty-five pages and the one you need opens in about four seconds, which is the only speed that has ever mattered behind a chair.
