DESCRIPCION (ingles)

That table has not had a meal on it since May.

Four chairs, four boxes, one on each seat, because I ran out of table. The labels are in marker because I write them while the kettle boils. The twine and the little tags and the scissors live there now. The two trays at the end are from Tuesday night.

And if you look through the doorway behind all of it you can see where we actually eat, which is the small round table in the kitchen, set for two, which is all it holds.

My name is Arlene Pruitt. I am fifty-six years old. Sapulpa, Oklahoma. My husband Lonnie runs a bread route and has been up at four in the morning for twenty-two years. Our daughter Jolene is thirty-four, in Tulsa, with a boy who just started kindergarten. Our son Wyatt is in Oklahoma City and calls on Sundays. There is a cat named Duchess who sleeps in the empty mould trays and has ruined two of them.

I have stood at a pharmacy register for twenty years. I still do.

So this is not a story about a woman who quit anything, and I want to say that up front because I am tired of reading those.

This is a story about a dining room table that I lost, on purpose, and have not asked for back.

Here is the part that I think is worth telling. In six months I have not once gone looking for an order. Not one. I have never run an advertisement, I do not have a website, there is no page with my name on it anywhere, and I have never put my face on anything.

Every box on those chairs is somebody who asked me.

That is the thing nobody told me was possible, and it is the reason I am sitting here taking a photograph of my own cluttered table like a crazy woman.

What is in the trays is scented stones. I had never heard of them either.

It is plaster. Plaster, a few drops of fragrance oil, and a silicone mould. You weigh the powder, you weigh the water, you stir it in a bowl until it looks like pouring cream, you fill the mould and you go to bed. In the morning there is a solid white stone in each well, smooth and cool and heavier than it looks, and it smells like a garden, and it keeps smelling for weeks. When it finally goes quiet you put two drops on the face and it comes back.

No oven. No wax. No flame. Nothing plugged into a wall.

I learned about it from a girl named Rochelle who covered the pharmacy counter for one summer while our regular tech was on leave. She was thirty. She mentioned on a slow Thursday that she made these at home, and I nodded the way you nod at a stranger's hobby, and then she brought me one on her second to last day and I held it in my hand at the register and I did not understand what I was holding.

I thought it was soap, which she thought was very funny.

My first batch, in March, was genuinely bad.

They came out of the mould with a grey cast to them, not white, and the backs were rough as a brick. One crumbled at the corner when I turned it over and left chalk on my fingers for two days. I had pushed a flower from a grocery store bunch into the top of one and inside of two weeks that flower was brown and curled and the whole thing looked like something a child brings home from church camp.

I called Rochelle to tell her I had wasted a Saturday, and she asked me what I had actually bought.

I had bought patching plaster from the hardware store, because plaster is plaster. It is not. There is more than one product and they are not the same material, and the one that gives you a white face and a hard edge is not the cheap bag on the end of the aisle.

Then she asked me how I measured the water, and I said with a measuring cup, which is the second thing I had wrong. A cup of powder packs differently every single time you scoop it. It goes on a kitchen scale, in grams, both of them, and then it comes out the same every time instead of coming out different every time.

And the last thing was that I pulled them out of the tray too early and called them dry. Drying and curing are two separate waits and the second one is the one that decides whether a stone looks handmade or looks homemade, and those are not the same word.

All of that was in a short guide by a woman named Emma Carter. Rochelle sent it to me that night. I read the whole thing in one evening sitting at the table you are looking at, back when we still ate on it.

The next batch came out white.

I sold the first ones to four women at church who wanted something for the teachers in May. Then the girl who does my hair wanted a basket by her register. Then a gift shop over on Dewey took a box in July and in September they stopped asking me for a box and started asking me how many I could do.

And then a woman who plans weddings, who I met exactly once, asked me for a hundred and twenty favors for an October wedding. Little stones with a dried flower set in the top, each one tied and tagged.

I said yes before I had worked out where I would put them. That is how the chairs happened.

Now here is the part I got wrong, and I am telling you because it was not a craft mistake, it was a business mistake, and it is the kind of thing I would have kept doing forever.

I quoted that wedding on the stones.

Just the stones. Then I bought a hundred and twenty kraft boxes and a hundred and twenty tags and a spool of ribbon out of my own pocket, and I sat at this table for three evenings with the television on tying every single one of them, and I charged nothing for any of it. Not the boxes, not the ribbon, not the three evenings.

I did not do that because I am generous. I did it because it never once occurred to me that the packaging was part of what she was buying. It is the whole reason it does not look homemade. It is the most valuable thing I do.

The guide has a price sheet in it and I had read right past that section, because I thought it was for people with real businesses. Packaging is a line on the sheet. It is not a courtesy. The spring wedding is quoted properly and she said yes to that one in one message, same as the first.

I am not going to sit here and tell you I got rich, because I did not, and you have heard enough of that from people who are lying.

I still work Monday to Friday behind that counter and I expect to for a while. What changed is that there is money in this house that nobody at a job decided to give me.

Three honest things, if you are the kind of woman who is already doing arithmetic.

You do not need to be crafty. I am not. I have never made one thing in my life that anybody wanted. It is weighing and stirring and waiting.

You do not need to be seen. Nobody has seen my face. People pick one up, they smell it, and they ask me what I want for it.

And you do not need a city. Sapulpa has one main street worth of stores and every single one of them sells something to somebody.

Emma's guide is dirt cheap and the link is below this post. It is short and it is specific and the two things that fixed everything for me, the scale and the curing, are the two things every free video skips.

Lonnie asked me in June when we were getting the table back.

He asked again in July.

He has not asked since, and last Tuesday he came in from his route, looked at the four chairs, and asked me when the next one comes in.

P.S. We eat faster now. The little table in the kitchen only holds two plates and there is nowhere to put your elbows, so dinner takes about eleven minutes, and honestly, after twenty-two years of a man who gets up at four, neither of us minds.

P.P.S. If somebody asks you for a big order, write down every single thing you are going to hand them, not just the thing you are going to make. The box, the tag, the ribbon, the evening you spend tying it. Price the handing over. That one mistake cost me three nights and I only noticed because somebody made me read a page I had skipped.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

That is the box I used to keep under the table so nobody would see it.

A cracked one. Two with little pinholes across the face like a saltine. One where the flower went brown on me. A few with a grey patch or a rough back. Nine of them in a shoebox on the wet grass, behind the cloth, next to my thermos.

I had a rule about that box, and the rule was that nothing imperfect goes on the table.

My name is Charlsie Dunn. I am fifty-six. Ozark, Alabama. My husband Earl drives a school bus and has driven the same route long enough that he is now driving the children of children he used to drive. Our boy Travis is thirty-six and in Dothan. Our girl Megan is thirty-one. There is a dog named Biscuit who is afraid of the vacuum and nothing else.

For nine years I cleaned rooms at a motel out by the highway, which is work I was good at and would not go back to.

The box came out from under that table in April, and it was not my idea.

I was crouched down after my thermos and I had the cloth pushed back with my shoulder, and a woman standing at my table looked past me and down and said, what are those.

I said, oh, those are the ruined ones.

She asked me how much I wanted for the box.

Not for one. For the box.

I did not have an answer. I said something about how that one is cracked and those two have holes in the top and the flower on that one has gone off, and she listened to me talk myself down for a while and then she said, honey, I am putting them in the bathroom of a cabin I rent out by the lake, and nobody is going to pick one up and inspect the back of it.

She took all nine and she asked me whether I would have more like that next month.

I want to explain why that was a bigger day for me than the day I sold out, because it was.

I had been deciding, by myself, in my own kitchen, what other people were allowed to want. Every stone that did not look like a photograph went in that box, and the box went under the table, and a few times a year I threw the whole thing in the bin. I have put forty or fifty stones in the garbage that I had already paid for.

Not because anybody complained. Nobody ever complained. Because I looked at a pinhole and felt caught.

What these are, in case this is the first time you are hearing of it, is plaster.

Plaster, fragrance oil, and a silicone mould. You stir it in a bowl, you pour it into the wells, you leave it alone overnight. The next day you peel the silicone back and there is a solid white stone in your hand, cool and surprisingly heavy, and it carries a scent for weeks. When it fades you put a couple of drops on the face of it and it comes back again.

No oven. No wick. No flame. Nothing to plug in. You can put one in a drawer and forget about it until you open the drawer.

My nephew's wife Kendra is the one who showed me. She is twenty-six and she sells things online that I do not entirely follow, and at a cookout two summers ago she handed me one and watched my face while I smelled it, which I now do to other people and understand completely.

My own first months were not good, and the trouble was not what I thought it was.

I thought I was bad at it. What I was actually doing was two specific things wrong, and neither one was the mixing.

The first was that I was pouring the fragrance oil straight into the wet plaster, because that is what every video I watched did. Oil in the bowl, stir, pour. What happens when you do that is most of it never ends up in the stone at all, and what is left sits on the outside and walks out of the room inside of a week. The scent goes on afterward, on the face, once the thing is actually dry, and there is an amount, not a splash.

The second one I did not find out until a customer told me. One of mine left an oily ring on the top of a woman's dresser. I was mortified. The back of a stone has to be sealed and the face has to be left open, so the scent has one direction to go and only one. Nobody had ever said the word sealed to me.

Both of those were in a short guide by a woman named Emma Carter, which is what Kendra had learned from and what she sent me the same night I asked her. It took me one evening to read. It is not a course and there is nothing to attend.

After that I stopped making bad ones very often. But I kept the box, out of habit, under the cloth, until the woman with the rental cabin.

Here is the honest part.

I am not going to tell you this made me rich. It did not. Earl still drives his route, I pour on Wednesday nights and I pack on Sunday afternoons with the ballgame on, and the money this brings in is not life changing money.

What it is, is mine, and there is nobody I have to ask.

And I am not telling you the fair is where the money is either, because it mostly is not. The fair is where people find out you exist. The woman with the cabin called me twice more. A gift shop owner took my number off that table. A woman planning her daughter's shower found me there and ordered for the shower and then for Christmas.

Three things I would say, if you are wondering whether you could.

Nobody is grading you. I spent a year believing a pinhole was a disqualification and the whole time there was a market for the ones I was hiding.

You do not have to be out front. I have never been on camera and I do not intend to be. People smell them and they buy them.

And you do not have to be artistic. I cleaned motel rooms. There is no art in this. There is a scale, a bowl, a mould and a night of waiting.

The guide is dirt cheap and the link is below this post. What it mostly gave me was the order to do things in, which nothing free had.

The box is on top of the table now.

It says SECONDS - ASK ME across the lid in marker, in Megan's handwriting, because she has better handwriting than me.

It is usually the first thing on that table to empty out, and a woman said to me in September, holding a cracked one up to the light, that this is the only table at the whole fair where you can tell a person actually made the things.

P.S. There is one I never sold and never will. The very first stone I ever made, split right down the middle, grey as a sidewalk. It is on the windowsill over my kitchen sink and it has no smell at all and I look at it every time I do the dishes.

P.P.S. Two things about seconds, since almost nobody sells them. Put one round price on the whole box so there is no negotiating. And do not explain the flaw before they ask you about it. I talked myself out of money for a solid year by apologising for a hole nobody had noticed yet.
