DESCRIPCION (ingles)

The letter has been on my kitchen table for nine days and I have read it maybe forty times.

It is one page. It is not even a nice letter. It is the kind a computer prints, with my name spelled in capitals and a reference number longer than my phone number. There is one line in the middle of it that says PAID IN FULL, and that is the only part of it I care about.

The grey envelope next to it is the old one. That is the envelope I used to put the payment in. Nine years of my handwriting on the front of it.

The stone sitting on top of the letter is one of mine. I put it there to hold the page flat and then I left it there, and now it looks like I staged it, and I suppose at this point I did.

My name is Imogene Bratcher. I am sixty years old. Washington, Indiana. My husband Carl is sixty-three and has run a forklift at the same grain elevator since he was twenty-four. Our daughter Shelly is thirty-eight, in Evansville, two boys. Our son Dean is in Terre Haute and calls on Sunday nights, usually from his truck. There is a cat named Pepper who is not allowed on the table and who is on the table in half the pictures I take.

I have worked a packing line at a food plant for twenty-two years. Second shift. I still do.

So let me get the usual part out of the way first, because I am tired of reading these and finding out at the bottom that the woman quit her job and moved to Florida.

I did not quit anything. I will be on the line Monday at two.

What happened is that a debt I had been carrying since 2019 is closed, and I closed it myself, in about seven months, with something I make on this table after my shift.

I am not going to make a sad story out of the debt. Everybody my age has one. Mine was medical, mine was not anybody's fault, and the number never moved much no matter what I threw at it, because that is how those work. For years the plan was that someday there would be extra. There is never extra. Anybody who has ever had a payment knows exactly what I mean.

Here is the part I actually want to tell you, because it is the part nobody told me.

I did not go looking for this. I was not hunting for a side hustle, I hate that phrase, and I never once typed "how to make money from home" into anything.

A girl at the plant named Tasha brought in a little white stone in her coat pocket and set it on the break room table on her break. Twenty-nine years old, works the bagger two spots down from me. She had made it. She was making them for her cousin's wedding, a hundred and twenty of them, and the cousin was paying her for it.

I picked it up because I thought it was soap.

It was plaster. Plaster and fragrance oil, poured into a silicone mould, left overnight on her counter. No kiln. No flame. Nothing you have to stand over. It smelled like a store, not like a craft fair, and it kept smelling like that for weeks, which I did not believe until mine did it too.

And I want to be plain about something, because the women who sell these online never are: it is obviously plaster in a mould. That is what it is. People buy them anyway, and they pay shop prices for them, and it took me a good while to stop being surprised by that.

What they are buying is that it is handmade and it came from here and it is not another candle.

Now I will tell you the ugly middle, because the ugly middle is the whole reason I am writing this.

My first three trays were homemade. Not handmade. Homemade. Homemade is what you give away to be polite about. Handmade is what somebody hands you money for. Nobody tells you where the line between them is, and the line is not in the recipe.

Mine came out of the mould with a chalky, open surface, little pinholes all across the face like the top of a cake, and edges you could rub away with your thumb. The scent was strong the first day and nearly gone by the weekend. I gave six of them to my sister and she put them in a drawer, which is where you put something you do not want to say is bad.

The two things that were wrong with my stones had nothing to do with the recipe.

The first was the surface. The side that faces the air while it sets is the side everyone looks at, and it has to be worked, not left alone. The second was that I was taking them out of the mould when they felt hard, and hard is not the same as cured, and that is where the crumbling edge comes from. Both of those are a matter of timing, and timing is exactly what gets skipped in every free video I watched.

I bought the short guide Tasha used, which is dirt cheap and which I read in one sitting at this table. It has the base formula, it has the fragrance ratios that actually hold in plaster instead of flashing off in a day, it has the pricing, and it has a plain list of what to do when something comes out wrong, which is the part I used the most.

My fourth tray came out white, smooth and heavy. My sister took four of them out of the drawer and asked for more, and then asked if she could give them out at her work.

And here is my own mistake, the real one, the one that cost me actual time, and I am telling on myself so you do not do it.

For the first three months, every single dollar that came in went straight back out into more moulds and more fragrance oils. I wanted to have one of everything. I had eleven moulds and I was using three. I told myself I was investing.

I was not investing. I was shopping.

What changed everything was the most boring decision of my life: every batch, a fixed share comes off the top and goes to the payment first, before I buy one single thing. No percentage math, no spreadsheet, just that order of operations. Payment first, materials second, me third.

That is what closed the letter on my table. Not a big order, not a lucky month. The same decision, made eighteen or nineteen times in a row.

I have never run an advertisement. I do not have a website. There is no page anywhere with my name on it and I have never put my face on anything. I sell to the people around me, who tell the people around them, and the hardest part of my week is deciding which orders I can actually take.

Carl asked me yesterday whether the letter was going to stay on the table forever.

I told him no, it is going on the refrigerator.

He said that is worse, and then he got the magnet.

P.S. The grey envelope is in the drawer now, with the rubber band still on it. I could not throw it out and I am not going to pretend I know why.

P.S. If you take one thing from this, take the order of operations, because it costs nothing: decide today what share of every batch goes to the thing you are actually trying to pay off, and take it off the top before you buy another mould. You will get further in six months with three moulds and that rule than I got in three months with eleven and no rule.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

Two suitcases by the door and one of them is new.

The navy one has been to Charlotte and back about nine times and that is the whole list. The green one has the tag still on the handle because I bought it three weeks ago and I keep walking past it to look at it, which I know is ridiculous.

The drawing taped to the side is three stick people holding hands. My daughter mailed it to me. He is two, so the tape is mine.

The little white stone on the hall table with the tag on it is one of mine. It says for Nana because my daughter wrote that on it as a joke and then I decided I was taking it with me.

My name is Loveda Spain. I am sixty-one. Gaffney, South Carolina. My husband Dalton is sixty-three and has worked at a tire shop on the bypass since Reagan was president, his words, not mine. Our daughter Pauline is thirty-three and lives in Tacoma, Washington, which is as far from here as you can get and still be in the same country, because the Air Force sent her there and then she stayed. Our grandson is Beau. He is two years and four months old. There is a dog named Junie who will not be coming.

I have cooked in a school cafeteria for twenty-four years. Breakfast and lunch, same kitchen, same ovens.

I had never met Beau in person.

I am going to say that once, plainly, and then I am going to move on, because I did not write this to be felt sorry for. I have seen that child's whole life on a telephone screen. Every tooth. Both haircuts. That is just what it is when the plane is nine hundred dollars and there are two of you and the roof needs what the roof needs.

The flight is Thursday. We paid for both tickets out of what I made on this kitchen table since March.

Here is how it got started, and it was not my idea at all.

There is a girl in my kitchen at the school named Kennedy, twenty-three, who runs the dish line and is going to be somebody. Back in February she was making white stones with pressed flowers in them for her sister's wedding and she brought one in to show the ladies at the steam table.

I thought it was a bar of expensive soap. I was wrong twice in one morning because then I thought it was a candle.

It is plaster. Plaster and fragrance oil, poured into a silicone mould on a counter, left alone overnight. No kiln. No open flame. Nothing that can burn the house down while you sleep, which at my age is a real consideration. The scent lasts weeks and then it fades, and when it fades the people who bought one come and find you, which took me a while to understand is the best part of the whole thing.

Kennedy was getting paid for a hundred and sixty of them for that wedding. A dish line girl. On the side. In February.

I want to be honest about my first attempts, because the pretty ones you see online are nobody's first attempt.

Mine came out grey. Not white, grey, like a sidewalk. And the flowers I pressed into them turned brown inside the stone within a week, so what I had was a grey rock with a dead flower in it, and I want you to picture me holding that up in my kitchen at eleven o'clock at night.

Grey is water. It is too much water in the mix, and a scale from the dollar store fixes it forever.

Brown flowers are the other thing nobody mentions: the flower has to be properly dried and it has to go in at the right moment, not fresh, not pressed that same afternoon. Fresh flowers rot in plaster. It is the kind of detail that is obvious once somebody says it out loud and that no free video I watched ever said out loud.

The short guide Kennedy used is dirt cheap, I read it over two evenings, and it told me both of those in the first fifteen pages, along with the base formula, the blends that actually hold, how to price a set instead of giving away singles, and who to walk into first.

I am not going to pretend I quit the cafeteria. I go back the Monday after we land. I like my ladies and I like getting paid every two weeks and I am not somebody who gambles.

Now, my mistake, and it is a dumb one, and it cost me real money.

Two weeks before the trip I stopped taking orders. All of them. I had it in my head that it was better to disappoint nobody than to make anybody wait, so when a florist in town asked me for two dozen for the week I was gone, I said no, and when a woman from church asked about favours for a shower, I said I would talk to her in December.

That was fear, dressed up as being considerate.

Kennedy, who is twenty-three, told me what I should have done in about four seconds: put the date you are back on everything, take the orders anyway, and deliver the week you return. People are not in a hurry. They have been waiting on handmade since the beginning of time.

So I called the florist back. The two dozen are due the Tuesday after we get home, the shower favours are the week after that, and I have more work waiting for me on the other side of this trip than I had before I left, which is not how I thought any of this worked.

Pauline called last night to go over who is picking us up.

Before she hung up she said, you are actually coming.

And I said yes ma'am, the suitcases are by the door.

P.S. The tag is still on the green suitcase and it is staying on until Thursday. Dalton has offered to cut it off twice. He has been told.

P.S. Here is the free piece of advice, since it is the thing I got wrong: if you have something coming up, a trip, a surgery, a week of grandkids, do not stop. Put the date you are available again on your orders and take them anyway. The waiting does not cost you the customer. Saying no is what costs you the customer.
