NATIVE 8 - SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PHOTOS OF THE BACKS OF WOMEN'S HEADS
(variable de la native 1 / el objeto casero que prueba el problema de memoria)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist standing at her own station, looking down at her phone lying face up on the counter. The screen shows a camera roll grid of small square photos, every one of them the back of a woman's head photographed from behind, dozens of them, rows and rows, all napes and partings and no faces. Beside the phone on the counter: cutting shears, a carbon comb with a cracked handle, three sectioning clips, a dusting of cut hair, a half full water bottle gone cloudy. Warm overhead salon light, the shadow of her arm across the counter. No people, no faces, no brand names, no readable text or numbers anywhere. Shot on an older phone, slightly soft and grainy, a real worn station with scratched laminate, not a studio, not styled.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There are six hundred and forty photos of the backs of women's heads on my phone. I have been taking them for nine years and not one of them has ever told me how to do a haircut.

I want to explain why I took them, because I am fairly sure some of you have a version of this folder on your own phone.

My name is Marlys Dubiel. I am fifty-eight. I have been licensed since 1991 and I have rented the back chair in a two-chair shop on a side street in Alpena, Michigan, for nineteen years.

I am good at my job. I am putting that at the top because the rest of this reads like a woman running herself down, and that is not what this is.

Here is how the photos started.

In 2016 a woman sat down for her six-week cut and said, do it exactly like last time. And I stood there behind her and realized I could not have told you what I did last time. I knew it came out well. I could not have rebuilt it.

So I started photographing the back of every head before the cape came off. Nine years of that. Six hundred and forty.

They are useless, and it took me most of those nine years to understand why. A photo of a finished haircut is the result. The result is the only part of a haircut that cannot teach you the haircut.

Because the problem was never the cuts I do every week. Carol's crown, Jeanine's cowlick, the four women who have had the same bob from me since my son got married. Those live in my hands.

The problem is the one I do not do often.

She sits down happy. She has her phone out already and she turns it around. An angled bob with a longer front. A hush cut. A butterfly with the short pieces at the face.

I recognize it in half a second. I know exactly what it is. I have seen that cut four hundred times.

And in the four seconds it takes me to pick up my comb, my brain reaches for the one thing I need and comes back with nothing.

Where does the first section go. What am I holding the top at. Is the front over-directed or left where it grows. Where does the corner sit.

I learned it. I have done it. I just cannot get it out of my own head in the four seconds I have before I owe that woman a sentence.

So I say the sentence.

"You know what, with your growth pattern I would soften that a little."

And she says, whatever you think, Marlys.

Let me tell you about Rosalie.

Rosalie Kaczmarek. Forty-four. She drives a school bus, she has been in my chair since 2013, eight in the morning every fifth Wednesday, and she brings me zucchini in August whether I want it or not.

In January she sat down with her phone and asked me for a butterfly. The real one, with the short pieces at the front.

My stomach did the thing. I said the sentence. She got long layers.

She did not book in March. Or April.

In June I saw her at the Meijer on Bagley Street with the butterfly, done by somebody with eleven years less experience than me, four miles from my chair. She hugged me in the cereal aisle and asked about my knee.

She never complained. Women do not complain. They just stop.

And I want to be very clear about this, because I spent years calling it the wrong thing. That was not a skill problem. Put me in a classroom that afternoon with a mannequin head and a printed diagram and I would have cut that butterfly clean.

It was a recall problem. Standing behind her, in four seconds, with her watching my face in the mirror.

Memory under pressure is not something you fix by learning more. I know, because I kept trying to learn more.

I drove to Grand Rapids for a two-day class in 2015 and cut a beautiful shag there with a man standing at my shoulder saying good, now over-direct that. He does not live in Alpena. At home, alone, at ten in the morning, he is not there, and I never cut that shag again.

I watched videos. A video is somebody else's hands at their speed while mine are holding a comb, and by the time I scrub back to the part I needed I have already cut it.

I have two hundred and twelve things saved on Instagram. I cannot find one of them when a woman is in my chair.

I asked in a stylist group online and got thirty-one answers that contradicted each other and two women arguing about elevation underneath.

And I took my six hundred and forty photos, which tell me what it looked like when it was done and never once where the sections went.

None of it exists in the four seconds. That is the only moment of the day that counts.

Last spring the girl who rents the front chair, Kiara, who is twenty-five and three years licensed, stood looking at her phone for twenty seconds before a bixie.

I asked her, with more attitude than I am proud of, what she was reading.

She turned the phone around. One page. The shape drawn from the back. The sectioning pattern drawn right on the head. The elevation written as a number. Where the guide starts and whether it travels. The steps numbered, one through nine.

I asked her if it felt like cheating.

She looked at me and said, "You photograph the end. This is the middle. The middle is the part nobody ever shows you."

Fifty-eight years old and a girl of twenty-five handed me the sentence I had needed since 1991.

It is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. One page per cut, twenty-five of them. It is not a course. Nothing to finish, nothing to log into, nobody talking for eleven minutes before the first section, nobody above me.

Here is the honest part.

The first page I opened told me no. A long bob with a diagonal forward line, and I was going to over-direct the front the way I have over-directed it since the nineties. The page had the guide traveling from the nape with no over-direction at all. I stood in my own shop arguing with a page for about five seconds, then I did it the page's way.

It sat better than any of mine ever has, and it was a full inch different from how I have been doing it for nineteen years.

The second one was the butterfly. The one I had owed Rosalie since January. Fifty-five minutes, two looks at the page, on a woman who came in off a cancellation.

That is the whole story. Two cuts. One no and one yes.

What I would have been looking for, if I had known there was anything to look for:

One page, open in four seconds. Not a chapter and not a video with a scrub bar.
The back of the head drawn, with the sections on it. Not a photograph of the front in good lighting.
The elevation as a number, and where the guide starts, and whether it moves.
A numbered order, so I know what the second section is before I have finished the first.
The mistake printed beside the step where it happens, instead of explained to me afterwards.

Five things. It does all five, and it is dirt cheap, which I think about every time I remember the gas money to Grand Rapids.

Rosalie came back in September and asked for it again, grown out, and sat there telling me the butterfly was the first haircut anybody had ever stopped her in a parking lot about.

When I finished I reached for my phone out of habit, and she said, "You still take those?"

I said yes.

She said, "Take one. The other drivers keep asking me who does it."

There are six hundred and forty-one photos now.

Marlys D.
Licensed cosmetologist, 35 years
Alpena, Michigan

P.S. I still take the photos. I am not going to pretend I stopped, and I am not going to pretend they are a system. They are a scrapbook. The difference between a scrapbook and a reference is that a reference tells you what to do next, and in nine years mine never did once.

P.P.S. On reading something with a woman in the chair. I worried about this more than anything else, so here is what actually happens: I read it while she is still at the shampoo bowl, or while I am sectioning, which is the one moment every stylist alive is already looking down. Twenty seconds. In a year and a half not one woman has asked me what I was looking at. What she sees is her hairdresser taking a second before she starts, and there is nothing more reassuring than that in front of a mirror.

P.P.P.S. On what it is not. It is not Pinterest. I want to be blunt because that is exactly what I assumed. Pinterest is the finished head, which is the one thing I already had six hundred and forty of. What I never had was the back of the head with the sections drawn on it and the degree I am meant to be holding it at, which is the entire job.

P.P.P.P.S. On being too far into a career for this. I am thirty-five years in. The twenty-five-year-old who rents the chair in front of me has been doing it since she was licensed, and she has never once sent a woman down the road. I would rather be a fifty-eight-year-old woman reading a page than a fifty-eight-year-old woman saying the sentence.

NATIVE 9 - THE DIFFUSER IS STILL IN THE BAG
(variable de la native 5 / la frase que suena a honestidad y es una puerta que se cierra)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist standing in a small salon looking up at the top of a wall cabinet above her station. On top of the cabinet, pushed back against the wall, a hair dryer diffuser attachment still sealed in its clear plastic bag, dusty, the bag gone slightly yellow. Next to it a cardboard box with the flaps open, two unopened bottles of curl cream with the seals still on, a cheap plastic clamp stand. A thick layer of dust on everything, visible dust in the air, dim light from a window with bent blinds to the left, the phone shadow across the cabinet. No people, no faces, no brand names, no readable text or labels. Shot on an older phone, slightly soft and grainy, real salon with chipped paint and a water stain on the ceiling tile, not a studio, not styled.

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There is a diffuser on top of my cabinet that is still in the plastic bag it came in. I bought it in 2014.

I want to tell you about the sentence that kept it in that bag for twelve years, because it sounds like honesty, and it is not honesty, and I think a lot of you are using a version of it this week.

My name is Glenda Faye Pursifull. I am fifty-seven. I have been licensed since 1994 and I have worked the middle chair of a three-chair shop beside a tire place in Cookeville, Tennessee, for twenty years.

I am a good hairdresser. I am very good on fine hair. Notice how pleasant that sentence is, because I built it on purpose and then I lived inside it for two decades.

My sentence was, "I'll be honest with you, honey, curly is not really my thing."

Say it out loud. It sounds like a woman being straight with you. It sounds like taste, or specialty, or knowing your own limits, which is supposed to be a virtue.

It is a door. And every time you say it you pull it a little further closed, and after enough years the door is a wall, and the wall is who you are in that town.

It never happens all at once.

In 2015 I sent one woman to somebody else. Then I stopped taking curly layers. Then anything with a real wave pattern through the mid-lengths. Then the ones who came in dry and asked me to cut it dry, because that frightened me more than anything. Then the mothers who brought their daughters, which is two heads and sometimes three.

Each one on its own is nothing. You are busy. She is young. There is a woman in Crossville who is better at it.

Add them up over twenty-two years and the cuts I do not do are not a gap in my book. They are my reputation. There are women in this county who have never once thought of me for anything that is not straight.

Let me tell you about Nadine.

Nadine Alcorn. Thirty-six, dental assistant, in my chair since 2017, and the kind of woman who texts you a photo of her kid's report card.

Two Februaries ago she came in with Rylee, who was eleven, and both of them have the same hair. Real curl. She wanted curly layers for herself and something shaped for her daughter, and she had three pictures saved and she had the words for it.

I said my sentence.

She said that was fine, took a trim on the ends, tipped me, and asked after my mother.

She found a woman in Crossville. Forty minutes each way, and she goes every ten weeks, and so does Rylee, and so does her mother on a Friday.

I did not lose a haircut. I lost a woman, her daughter, her mother, and whoever she talks to at that dental office, which in a town this size is the whole waiting room.

Here is what I finally understood, and it is not what I had been telling myself since the nineties.

It was never that I could not cut curly hair. My hands are thirty-two years into this and they are good.

It is that curly hair is not a hair type. It is a different set of rules.

Where the weight sits. Whether you cut it wet at all. Natural distribution instead of the tension I put on everything. How much it is going to come up when it dries, which is not a guess, it is a number you plan for.

And I did not have those rules written down anywhere. Not in my head, not on paper, nowhere. So I cut curly hair the way I cut straight hair, wet and stretched and even, and watched it spring up two inches and sit wrong, and I concluded that I was not a curly person.

I was not wrong about the result. I was wrong about the reason, for twenty-two years.

I did try. I want that on the record.

I drove to Nashville for a class in 2016. There was a model with beautiful even curl and an instructor saying now follow the curl family, and I did, and it was lovely, and that man has never once stood behind my chair in Cookeville.

I watched videos, which are somebody else's hands at their speed while mine are holding a comb and a spray bottle.

I bought a big book of curly hairstyles. Every page was a finished head, photographed from the front, lit by somebody who does that for a living. It is the result. Everybody can see the result. Nobody shows you the back of the head with the sections drawn on it, which is the actual job.

And I bought product. A whole shelf of curl cream, which is what you buy when you do not know what you are doing and want to feel like you are doing something.

What moved me was not any of that.

The girl who rents the chair by the door, Rosalia, twenty-nine, cut a curly long bob dry in forty minutes while I pretended not to watch.

I asked her where she got certified for that.

She said she was not certified for anything. She turned her phone around and said, "I have a reference. I read it before I start and I read it again before I touch the front."

One page. The shape from the back and the side. The sectioning drawn on the head. Dry or wet, said plainly. Where the guide starts and whether it travels. Steps in order. And the mistake printed next to the step where people make it.

She had pages for natural distribution and for shrinkage, which are two words I had been working around since 1994 without ever learning them properly.

It is called The Women's Cutting Atlas. One page per cut, twenty-five of them, and a block of them for curl and texture. Not a course. Nothing to finish, nothing to sign up for, nobody checking on me.

The honest part.

The first thing it told me was no.

I had a woman with a real wave pattern and I was going to do what I always do, which is wet it down, comb it flat and cut an even line. The curly page had it dry, in its own distribution, worked through curl by curl, with length left at the front for what comes up when it dries.

And the mistake printed beside the step was the exact thing I have done since the nineties: cut it wet and even, and let the shrinkage decide the shape instead of deciding it myself.

I stood in an empty shop on a Monday arguing with a page. Then I did it the page's way, on my own niece, who agreed to be the guinea pig for a tank of gas.

It dried where I put it. Twenty-two years and I had never once had that happen.

The first paying one was Pam Suddarth, fifty-two, who has worn the same bun since her husband's funeral because, in her words, loose it looks like a thunderstorm. Curly long bob, fifty minutes, two looks at the page. She touched the back of her neck and said, well, Glenda.

If I had known what to look for, here is what I would have wanted:

The shape drawn from the back and the side, with the sections on it, instead of a photograph of a girl in perfect light.
Wet or dry, said straight out, and where the guide starts.
The elevation as a number, and whether that guide travels or stays.
An order I can follow with wet hands, on a phone propped against a mirror, in under a minute.
What goes wrong, printed beside the step where it goes wrong, not explained to me after I have already done it.

Five things. It does all five, and it is dirt cheap, which is a hard thing to sit with when I think about the Nashville gas money and a shelf of curl cream I bought instead of knowing anything.

The diffuser came out of the bag in March.

Rosalia saw it on my station and said, "Twelve years in a bag, Glenda," and I told her to mind her own chair.

It is not on top of the cabinet anymore. It lives in my drawer with the clips, and it has been used forty or fifty times since the spring.

Rylee Alcorn has an appointment with me on the fourteenth. Her mother booked it. She did not say anything about it on the phone and neither did I, and I will be reading that page while the girl is still at the shampoo bowl.

Glenda P.
Licensed cosmetologist, 32 years
Cookeville, Tennessee

P.S. If you have a sentence like mine, I am not going to stand here and call you a liar. "I don't do curly." "I don't do short." "I don't really do men." Mine was not a lie. It was a true description of a result I kept getting for a reason I never looked up, and I let it become a personality.

P.P.S. On whether curly needs a certification. I thought it did, and that belief cost me twelve years and one entire family. A certificate is for a wall. What I needed was the rules on one page in the four seconds before I pick up my shears, and nobody in thirty-two years had ever put them in front of me that way.

P.P.P.S. On reading it with her sitting right there. She is in the chair, the hair is wet or it is dry, and you are looking down at her head anyway. Twenty seconds at the shampoo bowl, twenty seconds before the front. In seven months no woman has asked me what I was looking at, and the one who noticed said her last girl never checked anything, and she did not mean it as a compliment to her last girl.

P.P.P.P.S. On what it is not. It is not a product line and it is not somebody's named method with a logo. There is nothing to buy afterwards and nobody emails you. It is twenty-five pages, the one you need opens in about four seconds, and the curl pages are the reason I am writing this instead of sending another woman to Crossville.

NATIVE 10 - SHE ASKED FOR A HUSH CUT AT TWENTY TO TEN ON A TUESDAY
(variable de la NATIVE 1 de la cuenta / corta, escena en el sillon, sin credencial)
=====================================================================

PROMPT DE IMAGEN

POV phone photo, 3:4 vertical, taken by a stylist standing at her station looking down at the counter in front of her mirror. Her phone is propped upright against the base of the mirror, the screen showing a simple hand-drawn style line diagram of the back of a head with sectioning lines and small arrows on it, no words readable. On the counter: cutting shears open, a carbon comb, four sectioning clips, a spray bottle, a few long cut hairs across the laminate. DESCRIPCION (ingles)

A woman sat down in my chair at twenty to ten on Tuesday, turned her phone around and said, "Can you do a hush cut?"

I said, "Oh, that is a gorgeous cut." Inside my head everything went quiet in the worst possible way.

I had never done one. Not on a person.

If you cut hair you know that exact second. Your face doing one thing and the inside of your head doing another. Where does the first section go. What am I holding the top at. Do I start at the crown or the fringe. And she is sitting there trusting you, telling you about her weekend, with no idea.

For years I had two ways out of that second.

The first was talking her out of it. "Honestly, with your density I would keep a bit more length through here." Sometimes that was true. Most of the time it meant I did not know how to do what she had asked me for.

The second was booking her for the following week so I could watch tutorials the night before, and then never finding the forty seconds in the video where they actually explain the sectioning.

So I stayed with the same four cuts I know in my sleep. Safe. And every time I steered a woman away from what she came in for, I lost a little piece of her, and a few times I lost all of her to the shop down the road.

What changed is almost embarrassing. It is a visual atlas of women's cuts that lives on my phone.

That is what is propped against my mirror in the photo. One page per cut. How to section it, where the guide goes, the elevation for each zone, which direction you are cutting, the steps in order, the mistake people make printed right next to the step where they make it, and a tip at the end. Hush cut, wolf, shag, butterfly, bixie, pixie, graduated bob, long bob, curtain fringe.

No scrolling through half an hour of video with shears in my hand. You open the page for the woman in front of you and you follow the order.

So on Tuesday, instead of my sentence, I said, "Let me get set up," and I opened the hush page. Sections. Guide at the front. Elevation through the top. Connect the zones. Soften the ends. Step by step, while she told me about her sister's wedding.

She photographed the back of her own head before she stood up. She rebooked for seven weeks and asked if I had room for her sister on the same morning.

I sat in my car after my shift and did a piece of arithmetic I did not enjoy. Not the cut I did on Tuesday. All the ones I did not do. Every woman I sent somewhere else with something safer was a full service walking out of my own door, and she took her colour and her blowout with her when she went.

Now I say yes. Three cuts I used to quietly avoid are back on my menu, and I am glad when somebody brings me a photo instead of nervous.

It is not magic and it does not replace practice. It is the thing I wish somebody had handed me on my first day on the floor: a clear map I can check in seconds with a woman already in the chair.

It was dirt cheap, one time, and it came with five extra guides I use more than I expected. Fringes and bangs. How to diagnose a cut that went wrong. Shears and tools. Seamless layers. Which cuts suit which face shape. I keep it on my phone and I printed the four pages I use most and they live in my station drawer. There is a seven-day guarantee, so if it does not fit the way you work you ask for your money back and that is the end of it.

If you have ever said "oh, I love that one" with your stomach somewhere around your knees, the link to the Atlas is below this post.

Next week I have a wolf and a bixie booked. I am looking forward to both of them.
