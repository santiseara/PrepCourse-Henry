There are nine messages on that phone that I have not answered yet, and all nine of them want to give me money.

I propped the phone against the sugar jar so I could take the picture, and then I looked at the picture and thought, well, that is the whole thing right there. Twelve stones on the towel. Nine people waiting. That is not a business plan, that is just arithmetic that does not work in my favour.

My name is Wanda Sue Kiefer. I am fifty-seven. Hamilton, Alabama. My husband Dale hauls grain and has had the same bad shoulder since 2011. Our daughter Reba is thirty-three and lives twenty minutes away and calls while she is driving, which is the only time she calls. There is an old beagle named Rooster who sleeps under this exact counter and who has never once moved for anybody.

I pass medication in a nursing home. Eighteen years. I still do, three days a week, and I am going to keep doing it, so if you are waiting for the part where I tell you I walked out of there, it is not coming.

What I want to tell you about is the nine messages, because a year ago the idea that nine people in this county would be waiting on me for anything would have made me laugh out loud.

I did not look for this. I want to be clear about that, because every one of these posts says the woman was desperate and typed something into her phone at two in the morning. I was not desperate. I was tired, which is different, and I was annoyed in that specific way where every month is fine and no month is ever better than fine.

A girl at work named Marlee brought it in. Twenty-six, works the hall across from mine, has a baby and a boyfriend in the Guard. She had a little white stone in her scrub pocket and she set it on the counter at the nurses' station because somebody asked what the smell was.

I thought it was a bath bomb. I honestly did.

It was plaster. Plaster and fragrance oil, poured into a silicone mould, left out overnight. No kiln, no flame, nothing you have to watch. She was making them for her sister-in-law's shop in Winfield and she said the words "they keep taking them" in the same tone you would use to say it might rain.

So let me do the honest part early, because the women who sell these online never do it at all.

It is plaster in a mould. That is what it is. It is not a craft, it is not a calling, it is not some ancient technique. You mix a powder with water and you pour it into a shape and you wait. People buy them anyway. People pay shop prices for them. It took me months to stop being surprised by that and I am still a little surprised by it.

Here is what they are actually buying, as far as I can tell. Their house smells like something nice for weeks with nothing burning and nothing plugged in, and a person they know made it, and it is not another candle. That is it. That is the entire reason.

Now the part nobody tells you, which is the part that cost me the first two months.

My first stones were bad. Not ugly. Bad in a way I could not name, which is worse, because you cannot fix what you cannot name. I would set one next to the one Marlee gave me and mine looked like a copy of hers.

Two things were wrong and neither one was the flowers or the colour.

The first was weight. Mine were light. A stone that is light feels cheap in the hand and the person holding it decides what it is worth before they ever smell it, and they decide fast. That is water. Too much water, every time. I was eyeballing it with a measuring cup like I was making gravy. Once I started weighing it, the same mould gave me something that landed in a person's palm with a little bit of authority, and I did not have to say anything about quality because the stone said it.

The second was reach. Mine smelled lovely if you picked it up and put it near your face. Hers smelled lovely when you walked into the room. Those are two completely different products, and only one of them gets reordered. That was fragrance load and when I added it, not what fragrance I bought, which is what I had assumed and what I had wasted money on.

Nobody tells you either of those things. The videos all stop at the pretty part, where she lifts it out of the mould and turns it in the light and the music swells. Nobody films the part where you hand somebody a stone and watch their face not change.

Once those two things were right, I stopped being a woman with a hobby.

I gave some away. That is all I did. I never sold anything to anybody for the first several weeks. I put one in a gift bag for a birthday, I left two at the nurses' station, I gave one to the woman who cuts my hair and one to Reba's mother-in-law. And then the phone started.

Here is my own stupid mistake, and I am telling you so it does not eat two weeks of your life the way it ate two of mine.

I answered every message the minute it came in and I made every order by itself. Somebody wanted six, I made six. Somebody wanted a box, I made a box. I was standing in this kitchen four and five nights a week pouring tiny batches like a short-order cook, and I was exhausted, and I was no further ahead.

The tray holds twelve. The mix is the same amount of work for one as it is for twelve. All I had to do was tell people a day. That is it. That is the whole fix. "I pour on Tuesday, I will have them Thursday." Not one person has ever complained about waiting. Not one. I had invented a problem out of being afraid to make anyone wait, which is a very particular kind of female stupidity that I recognise in myself and have mostly stopped.

I turned my own sign around at twenty to twelve and wrote on the back of it with a marker I borrowed from the woman selling dog treats.

That cloth in the picture is my table. You can see the clean squares where the trays were, because the cloth had been in a tote bag all winter and the trays were sitting on the creases. My name is Treva Lively. I am fifty-five, Checotah, Oklahoma. Two grown boys, one in Muskogee, one in Tulsa who we see at Christmas and mean to see more. I work the counter at an auto parts store nine years this January, which means I have spent nine years being asked by men if I am sure about the part number.

I had never sold anything to a stranger in my life before this year. Not Avon, not Tupperware, not raffle tickets. Nothing.

Let me get the honest part out of the way, because I am not going to spend four paragraphs pretending this is magic.

What I make is plaster poured into a silicone mould with fragrance oil in it. That is all it is. It sits overnight, it comes out white and smooth, and it smells like a nice shop for about two months. There is no kiln, no flame, no machine, nothing to plug in. The whole operation lives in a plastic tote under the spare room bed.

People buy them anyway, and they pay real money for them, and the first few times it happened I felt like I was getting away with something. I have gotten over that.

My daughter-in-law Shanda is the one who put me onto it. I made the first batch three weeks before that fair and they were, to be blunt about it, not sellable, and the reason is not what you would guess.

They were not ugly. Individually, every one of them looked fine. The problem only showed up when I set twelve of them out in a row, which of course is the only way anyone ever sees them.

They did not match. Some were brighter white and some were a little grey, some had a satin face and some were dull, because I had mixed each batch by feel and poured them over four different evenings. Twelve stones that do not look like sisters do not look handmade. They look like mistakes. A person walks up, picks up two, compares them without even realising they are doing it, puts both down and keeps walking. I watched that happen in my own kitchen with Hollis, who was not even trying to shop.

The other thing was yellowing. Two of mine had gone faintly tea-coloured on the top by the second week and I thought something had gone wrong with my water. It was the fragrance. Certain ones do that in plaster, and vanilla is the famous offender, and there is a way around it that takes ten seconds and zero extra money, and I had no idea because every video I had watched was filmed on day one, when everything on earth still looks fine.

Once I fixed those two things, the trays looked like a shelf in a store instead of a table at a fair. Same mould. Same fragrance. Same woman.

By eleven-forty there was nothing on the table and a woman was asking me if I would do twenty for her daughter's baby shower, and I had no bags, no cards, no way to hold a place for anybody. I turned the sign around so I would stop having the same conversation, and I wrote SOLD OUT - TAKING ORDERS on the back of it because it was the only blank surface I had, and the notebook in the picture is what I used to write people down. That column kept going onto the next page.

A gift buyer does not want an object. They want the thing to already be finished so they can hand it over. I sold out anyway because it was September and the table was full, but I know exactly what I left in that notebook instead of in my hand, and it was not a pricing problem and it was not a product problem.
