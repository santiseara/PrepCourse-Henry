DESCRIPCION (ingles)

Nine years ago I started writing down what I did to every head that sits in my chair. There is a column on the right side of my book for it. Yesterday I turned back through it and the same word is in that column about four hundred times.

I am going to tell you what the word is in a minute. First I want to tell you who I am, because the rest of this only makes sense if you know I am not a beginner.

My name is Charlene Ostrander. I am fifty-seven. I have been licensed since 1992 and I have had the second chair at a three-chair shop on Sandusky Avenue in Bucyrus, Ohio, for twenty-two years. I keep a paper book because I do not trust the app, and because my hands think better when they are holding a pen.

I am good at my job. I am putting that at the top because the rest of this is going to read like a woman running herself down, and that is not what this is.

The word is trim.

Four hundred trims. Root touch-ups. The same bob on the same four women since my daughter's wedding. All of it work I can do with my eyes closed, which is exactly why I can do it.

And here is the part I have never said out loud in a shop.

The cuts that pay the most in this town are the ones with a name on them. A hush cut. A wolf. A butterfly with the short pieces around the face. A bixie. Women walk in asking for them by name now, with the picture already open on the phone, which is not how it was ten years ago.

I know those cuts. I have been taught every one of them. I have done most of them more than once.

What I cannot do is pull the order out of my own head in the four seconds between her turning the phone around and me owing her a sentence.

Where does the first section go. What elevation am I holding the top at. Is the front over-directed or left where it falls. Where does the corner sit so it does not shelf when it dries.

I know all of it. I just cannot get it out standing up with a woman watching me in the mirror.

So I say the sentence. Every stylist reading this knows the sentence.

"You know what, honey, with your density I would go a little softer than that."

And she says, whatever you think, Char.

And I write trim in the column.

Let me tell you about Deneen.

Deneen Hollobaugh is forty-one, a dental hygienist, two kids, and she had been in my chair every six weeks for nine years. Her husband comes to me. Her mother came to me until she passed.

Last February she sat down with her phone already out and asked for a wolf cut. She had been growing it for it. She said she had been thinking about it since Christmas.

I looked at it for maybe two seconds. I knew the cut. I could see the shape of it in my head, the short collapsed layers at the crown and the long pieces underneath, and I could not for the life of me tell you where I was supposed to start on her.

So I softened it. Long layers and a bit of face framing and a blow dry that made it look like something.

She said she loved it. She paid. She tipped me.

In May I was in the Kroger on Marion Road and I saw her in the dairy aisle with a wolf cut. A good one. Somebody had done the disconnection properly.

She was kind about it. She said hi, we talked about her son's baseball. She never once said anything about her hair, and I have thought about that more than I would like to admit.

I did not lose a haircut that day. I lost Deneen. Her colour, her blow dries before weddings, her husband's clipper cuts, her six weeks for the next however many years. Eleven minutes of saying one soft sentence.

And I want to be clear about something, because I think this is where a lot of us get it wrong.

She did not leave because I am a bad stylist. She left because I could not remember a sequence standing up.

Those are not the same problem, and I spent about four years treating them like they were.

Here is everything I tried, and why each one failed.

Classes. I have taken them. You drive two hours, you pay for a day, you cut on a model in a room with good light and a teacher two feet away, and you go home able to do it. Six weeks later, with a real client and a real schedule, the order is gone. A class teaches your hands and then your hands forget.

Tutorials. There is a video for every cut ever invented. There is not a video you can check in the eleven seconds you have before you start, and nobody is standing there with wet hands scrubbing a twenty-two minute video for the part where she sections the crown.

Screenshots. I have a folder. You all have the folder. It is three hundred pictures of finished hair. A picture of a finished haircut is the result, and the result is the only part of a haircut that cannot teach you the haircut.

Asking the girls. Fine for one cut. You cannot build a menu out of leaning over somebody else's chair.

Writing my own notes. This is the one that hurts. I did write them, in the back of that same book. They are useless, because I wrote them the way you write something you already understand, and six months later I was not that woman anymore.

So that is where I was. A full book, a short menu, and a column full of the word trim.

Then Tamsin started renting the front chair.

She is twenty-six. She has been licensed four years, which is nothing, and she was taking cuts I was quietly steering women out of. Not because she is faster than me. I have better hands than that girl and we both know it.

In October I watched her take a bixie on a woman with fine hair and a double crown, which is a cut that can go very wrong, and before she picked anything up she looked at her phone for about ten seconds. Then she put it face down and cut for forty minutes and it was lovely.

I asked her what she was looking at. I was expecting a video.

She said, it is one page. Sectioning, elevation, guide, the order, and the mistake that ruins it. She said, I am not better than you, Char. I just read it first.

She sent me the link that night.

I am going to tell you exactly what happened the first week, including the part that is not flattering, because I have read enough of these to know when somebody is selling me something.

The first thing it did was tell me no.

A woman wanted a bixie, fine hair, the same double crown. I opened the page and the mistake to avoid was printed right there under the sequence, and it was precisely the thing I was about to do to the back of her head. I did not do the bixie. I did a soft short layered shape instead and I told her why, out loud, with the page open.

She booked back at six weeks.

The yes came on the Thursday. A hush cut on a woman I would have talked out of it a month earlier. I read the page at the basin while her conditioner sat, I checked the elevation twice standing behind her, and I was done in fifty minutes and it was clean.

I have been using it since. The column in my book has other words in it now.

What it is: one page per cut. Twenty-five of them. The technical data at the top, the sectioning map, the numbered steps, who it suits, the common mistake and a final check. It is a PDF. It lives on my phone and there is a printed copy in the drawer under my station because my reading glasses are a situation.

It is to check, not to study. That distinction is the whole thing, and it took me a year of my life to understand it.

Before you buy anything, here is how I would decide, in your position.

One. Can you check it mid-service, in under a minute, with wet hands and somebody watching you. If it is a video, no.

Two. Does it show you the middle of the haircut, not the end of it. Photos of finished hair are not help.

Three. Does it tell you where it goes wrong, before you get there.

Four. Is it the cuts people are actually asking you for this year, by name.

Five. Can you use it on your next client, not after a course with a date on it.

That is it from me. I am a hairdresser in Bucyrus, not an expert on the internet. I am fifty-seven years old and I had a column in a book that said trim four hundred times, and the fix was not talent, and it was not a better pair of shears, and it was not another class.

It was having the order in front of me at the moment I needed it.

P.S. Deneen came back in September. She asked for a shag. I did the shag.

P.S. If you have the folder of three hundred photos of the backs of heads on your phone, I am not making fun of you. I had nine years of the same word in a column. We are the same woman.

P.S. It is dirt cheap, it downloads the minute you get it, and there is a seven day guarantee, which I used exactly zero of.
