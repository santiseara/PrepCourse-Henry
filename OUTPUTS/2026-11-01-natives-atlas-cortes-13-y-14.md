NATIVE 13 - ELEVEN NAMES ON A ROLL OF RECEIPT PAPER

DESCRIPCION (ingles)

Every December for the last four years I have written a name on the back of a roll of receipt paper that is taped inside my drawer.

Last Christmas Eve there were eleven names on it.

I will tell you what the list is in a minute, because it is not what you think and it took me two years to even admit I was keeping it.

First, who I am, because the rest of this only makes sense if you know I am not some girl six months out of school.

My name is Earlene Pankey. I am fifty-nine. I have been licensed since 1989 and I have had the back chair in a two-chair shop on Bland Street in Bluefield, West Virginia, for nineteen years. I own my chair, my shears and my half of the rent, and I have cut hair through three recessions and whatever you want to call the last five years.

I am good at this. I am saying that at the top because what comes next reads like a woman tearing herself down, and that is not what this is.

The list is the women I sent away.

Not women I turned down. I have never in my life told a woman no. It is softer than that, and I think that is why it took me so long to see it.

She sits down in November or December with the phone already in her hand, because the fundraiser is on the twelfth and her son is getting married in April and her whole family will be in one room for the first time since 2019. She has had the picture saved since the summer. She turns it around.

A hush cut. A wolf. A butterfly with the short pieces around the face. A bixie. They ask for them by the name now, which is not how it was when I started.

And I look at that picture and I know the cut. I want you to hear that. I know what it is, I know what it should look like, I have been taught most of them at one point or another.

What I cannot do is pull the order of it out of my own head in the four seconds between her turning that phone around and me owing her an answer.

Where does the first section go. What elevation am I holding the top at. Is the front over-directed or left where it falls. Where does the corner sit so the whole thing does not shelf out when it dries.

So I say the sentence. Every woman reading this knows the sentence.

"You know what, hon, with your density I would go a little softer than that."

And she says, whatever you think, Earlene.

And I give her long layers and a blow dry that makes it look like something, and she pays, and she tips me, and I write her first name on that strip of paper when she is out the door, and I could not tell you why I started doing it.

Eleven names last December. Eleven women who came in asking for one thing and left with the thing I could do standing up.

Now here is the part that has nothing to do with feelings, and the part I want you to actually sit with.

Those six weeks before Christmas are the best-paying weeks of my entire year. Everybody in this trade knows that. The chairs are full, the deposit box is heavier, you work Saturdays you would not normally work.

And every one of those eleven women was willing to pay me for the expensive thing, and I sold her the cheap thing, in the only month of the year where she was never going to argue about the price.

That is not a bad Christmas. That is the same Christmas I have had four years in a row.

Let me tell you about Shelva Jean.

Shelva Jean Kirtley is forty-three, a pharmacy tech at the hospital, and she had been in my chair every six weeks for eleven years. Her mother comes to me. I did her daughter's hair for prom.

Two Decembers ago she sat down and asked for a butterfly. Her daughter was getting married in the spring. She had been growing it out for a year and she said so.

I looked at it for maybe two seconds, and I softened it. Long layers, some face framing, and honest to God it was a nice haircut.

She said she loved it. She paid. She tipped me twenty dollars.

In February I was in the Walmart on Leatherwood and she came round the end of the aisle with a butterfly. A proper one. Somebody had disconnected the top the way it is supposed to be disconnected.

She was lovely about it. We talked about the wedding for ten minutes. She never once mentioned her hair, and I have thought about that more than I would like to admit.

I did not lose a haircut that day. I lost Shelva Jean. Her colour, her mother, the wedding party, her six weeks for the next however many years. Eleven years of a woman, for one soft sentence that took me two seconds to say.

And I need you to hear the next part clearly, because this is where I think a lot of us go wrong.

She did not leave because I am a bad hairdresser. She left because I could not get a sequence out of my own head while standing up with her watching me in the mirror.

Those are two completely different problems, and I spent about four years treating them like they were the same one.

Here is everything I tried, and why every single one of them failed.

Classes. I have paid for them. You drive two and a half hours, you cut on a model in a room with good light and somebody standing at your elbow, and you drive home knowing it. Seven weeks later, with a real woman in your chair and a timer running in the next one, the order is gone. A class teaches your hands and your hands forget.

Tutorials. There is a video for every cut ever invented. There is no video you can check in the eleven seconds you have before you start, and nobody is scrubbing through twenty-two minutes with wet hands to find the part where she sections the crown.

Screenshots. I have the folder. You have the folder. Four hundred pictures of finished hair. A picture of a finished haircut is the end of it, and the end is the only part of a haircut that cannot teach you the haircut.

Asking the other girl. Fine for one cut. You cannot build a price list out of leaning over somebody else's chair.

Writing my own notes. That is the one that stings. I did write them, in a notebook, after classes. They are useless, because I wrote them the way you write something while you still understand it, and nine months later I was not that woman anymore.

So that is where I was last autumn. A full diary, a short price list, and eleven names on a roll of till paper.

Then Jaycie started renting the front chair.

She is twenty-three. Four years licensed, which is nothing, and she was taking cuts I had been quietly sending down the street for a decade. Not because she is better than me. My hands are better than that girl's and we both know it.

In October I watched her take a bixie on a woman with fine hair and a double crown, which is a cut that can go wrong in about four different ways, and before she touched anything she looked at her phone for maybe ten seconds. Then she put it face down on the counter and cut for forty minutes and it was lovely.

I asked her what she was looking at. I assumed a video.

She said, it is one page. Sectioning, elevation, guide, the order of it, and the mistake that ruins it. And she said, I am not better than you, Earlene. I just read it first.

She sent it to me that night.

I am going to tell you exactly how the first week went, including the part that does not flatter me, because I have read enough of these to know when I am being sold something.

The first thing it did was tell me no.

A woman wanted a bixie. Fine hair, same double crown. I opened the page and the mistake to avoid was printed right there under the sequence, and it was precisely the thing I was about to do to the back of her head. So I did not do the bixie. I did a short soft layered shape instead and I told her why, out loud, with the page open on the counter where she could see it.

She rebooked at six weeks.

The yes came on the Thursday. A hush cut on a woman I would have softened a month before. I read the page at the basin while her conditioner sat, I checked the elevation twice from behind her, and I was finished in fifty minutes and it was clean.

What it is: one page per cut, twenty-five of them. The technical data at the top, the sectioning map, the numbered steps, who it suits, the common mistake that ruins it and a final check. It is a PDF. It lives on my phone and there is a printed copy in the drawer under my station, because my reading glasses are a whole situation.

It is to check, not to study. That distinction is the entire thing and it took me a year of my working life to understand it.

Before you buy anything at all, here is how I would decide, standing where you are standing.

One. Can you look at it mid-service, in under a minute, with wet hands and a woman watching you.

Two. Does it show you the middle of the haircut. The end of it is already on your phone four hundred times.

Three. Does it tell you where it goes wrong before you get there, not after.

Four. Is it the cuts women are walking in and asking for by name this year.

Five. Can you use it on the woman sitting in your chair tomorrow, without a course with a date on it three months out.

That is me done. I am a hairdresser in Bluefield, not an expert on the internet. I am fifty-nine years old and I kept a list of eleven women in a drawer and the fix was not talent, it was not a better pair of shears and it was not another class with a hotel room attached to it.

It was having the order in front of me at the moment I needed it.

P.S. The six weeks that are starting right now are the best-paying weeks of your year. Whatever you are going to be able to do by the second week of December, you can already do today, or you cannot.

P.S. Shelva Jean came back in September. She asked for a shag. I did the shag, and I did not say the sentence.

P.S. It is dirt cheap, it downloads the minute you buy it, there are five extra guides with it and a seven day guarantee that I used exactly none of. The list is still taped in my drawer. I keep it where I can see it.
