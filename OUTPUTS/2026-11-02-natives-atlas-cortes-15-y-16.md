DESCRIPCION (ingles)

There is a photograph taped to the bottom corner of my mirror. It has been there since 2017 and the colour has gone out of it.

Every woman who has sat in my chair in the last nine years has looked at it. A lot of them have pointed at it.

It is the best haircut I have ever done, and I have never once been able to do it again.

Before I explain that, let me tell you who I am, because what comes next sounds like a woman running herself down and it is not that.

My name is Delphia Hausman. Everybody calls me Dell. I am fifty-seven. I have been licensed since 1992 and I have had the same chair in a three-chair shop on Mulberry Avenue in Muscatine, Iowa, for twenty-two years. I rent my chair, I own my shears, I have cut hair through two recessions, one divorce and whatever the last five years were.

I am good at this. I want that on the record before the rest of it.

Here is the thing about that photograph.

I did that haircut on a Thursday afternoon in March. It was a disconnected shape, heavy through the back, soft and broken up around the face. The woman cried in the chair, the good kind. I took the picture because I was proud of it, and I taped it to the mirror because I was proud of it.

And then I spent nine years not being able to do it on purpose.

I have done it badly. I have done things next to it. I have done a version of it that was perfectly nice and was not that. What I have never been able to do is stand behind a woman, look at that picture, and walk myself through the thing in the right order.

Because that afternoon I did not know the order either. I got there. There is a difference between getting there and knowing the road, and it took me most of my fifties to be honest with myself about which one that was.

Now let me tell you what it actually costs, because this is not a story about feelings.

A woman sits down. She has had the picture saved on her phone since the summer. Sometimes she does not even need her phone, she just points at my mirror.

A hush cut. A wolf. A butterfly with the short pieces around the face. A bixie. A proper disconnected shag. They ask for them by name now, which is not how it was when I started.

And I know the cut. I want you to hear that clearly. I know what it is, I know what it should look like, I have been taught most of them at one point or another over thirty years.

What I cannot do is pull the order of it out of my own head in the four seconds between her asking and me owing her an answer.

Where does the first section go. What elevation am I holding the interior at. Is the front over-directed back or left where it falls. Where does the corner sit so the whole thing does not shelf out the second it dries.

So I say the sentence. Every woman reading this knows the sentence.

"You know what, hon, with your texture I would go a little softer than that."

And she says, whatever you think, Dell.

And I give her long layers and a blow dry that makes it look like something. She pays. She tips me. She is perfectly happy walking out.

And here is the one that took me years to see: the days I do say yes, it costs me the other way. I take the cut, and then I decide it and execute it at the same time, standing up, with her watching me in the mirror. The forty-five minute slot runs an hour twenty. The woman booked behind her waits. I eat lunch at four o'clock, or I do not eat it.

That is the whole trap, and nobody warned me about it at school. You either sell her the cut you can do standing up, or you pay for the good one out of your own day.

Let me tell you about Arlys.

Arlys Petersen is forty-seven. She runs the front desk at the vet clinic on Houser Street, and she had been in my chair every six weeks for twelve years. I did her hair for her mother's funeral. I know what she drinks.

Two summers ago she sat down, pointed at the photograph on my mirror, and said, Dell, I have been looking at that thing for nine years, can I finally have it.

I looked at it for maybe two seconds, and I softened it. Long layers, a bit of face framing, and honest to God it was a nice haircut.

She said she loved it. She paid and she tipped me and we talked about her son the whole time.

In October I was in the Hy-Vee and she came around the end of the aisle with the haircut. The actual one. Somebody had disconnected the back the way it is supposed to be disconnected.

She was lovely about it. We talked for ten minutes and she never once mentioned her hair, and I have thought about that more than I would like to admit.

I did not lose a haircut that day. I lost Arlys. Her colour, her blow dries before anything that matters, her six weeks for however many years were left. Twelve years of a woman, for one soft sentence that took me two seconds to say.

And she did not leave because I am a bad hairdresser. She left because I could not get a sequence out of my own head while standing up with her watching me in the mirror.

Those are two completely different problems and I spent about nine years treating them like one.

Here is everything I tried, and why each one failed.

Classes. I have paid for plenty. You drive three hours, you cut on a model in a room with good light and somebody at your elbow, and you drive home knowing it. Six weeks later, with a real woman in the chair and a timer running in the next one, the order is gone. A class teaches your hands, and hands forget.

Tutorials. There is a video for every cut ever invented. There is no video you can check in the eleven seconds you have before you start, and nobody is scrubbing through twenty-two minutes with wet hands to find the part where she sections the crown.

Photographs. Well. I have a mirror with one taped to it and a phone with four hundred more. A photograph of a finished haircut is the end of it, and the end is the only part of a haircut that cannot teach you the haircut. It took me nine years and a faded picture to work that out.

Asking the girl in the next chair. Fine for one cut. You cannot build a price list out of leaning over somebody else's shoulder.

My own notes. That one stings. I wrote them after classes, in a notebook. They are useless, because I wrote them the way you write something while you still understand it, and a year later I was not that woman anymore.

So that was me last autumn. A full book, a short price list, a photograph on my mirror and a lunch I kept eating standing up at four o'clock.

Then Noelle started renting the middle chair.

She is twenty-five. Four years licensed, which is nothing, and she was taking cuts I had been quietly talking women out of for a decade. Not because she is better than me. My hands are better than that girl's and the two of us know it.

In September I watched her take a French bob on a woman with a strong wave, which is a cut that can go wrong in about four different ways, and before she touched anything she looked at her phone for maybe ten seconds. Then she put it face down on the counter and cut for forty minutes and it was clean.

I asked her what she was watching. I assumed a video.

She said, it is one page. Sectioning, elevation, guide, the order of it, and the mistake that ruins it. And then she said the thing I have not stopped thinking about: I am not better than you, Dell. I just read it first.

She sent it to me that night.

I am going to tell you exactly how the first week went, including the part that does not flatter me, because I have read enough of these to know when I am being sold something.

The first thing it did was tell me no.

A woman wanted a blunt French bob, same strong wave through the mid-lengths. I opened the page and the mistake to avoid was printed right there under the sequence, and it was precisely what I was about to do to the back of her head. So I did not do the blunt bob. I did a soft graduated shape that sat where her wave actually wanted to sit, and I told her why, out loud, with the page open on the counter where she could see it.

She rebooked before she left.

The yes came on the Friday, and you already know which cut it was. A woman pointed at my mirror. I read the page at the basin while her conditioner sat. I held the elevation it told me to hold, I checked the disconnection twice from behind her, and I finished in fifty-five minutes.

Nine years, and it took fifty-five minutes, and I was not guessing for one second of it.

What it is: one page per cut, twenty-five of them. Technical data at the top, the sectioning map, the numbered steps, who it suits, the common mistake that ruins it, and a final check. It is a PDF. It lives on my phone and there is a printed copy in the drawer under my station, because my reading glasses are a whole situation.

It is to check, not to study. That distinction is the entire thing and it cost me a decade to understand it.

Before you buy anything at all, here is how I would decide, standing where you are standing.

One. Can you read it in under a minute, mid-service, with wet hands and a woman watching you in the mirror.

Two. Does it show you the middle of the haircut. The end of it is already on your phone four hundred times.

Three. Does it tell you where the thing goes wrong before you get there, instead of after.

Four. Is it the cuts women are walking in and asking for by name this year, not the ones that were on the wall when you qualified.

Five. Can you use it on the woman sitting in front of you tomorrow morning, without a course with a date on it three months out.

That is me done. I am a hairdresser in Muscatine, not an expert on the internet. I am fifty-seven years old and the fix was not talent, it was not a better pair of shears, and it was not another class with a hotel room attached.

It was having the order in front of me at the moment I needed it.

P.S. Arlys came back in March. She asked for a shag, I did the shag, and I did not say the sentence.
